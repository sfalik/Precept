# Skills Rigor Evaluation

## Verdict

**Below-bar — by roughly one tier from where Precept's stated ambitions place it.** The skills are well above the level of an internal style guide and meaningfully better than informal "write a design doc" instructions. They carry several genuinely strong forcing functions — citation-with-excerpt being the best one — that are absent from most engineering shops. But measured against the comparators the project itself invokes (PL theory, formal methods, the catalog-as-spec ambition, "domain integrity engine" framing) and against the comparators in this evaluation's brief (Rust RFC, TC39, formal-methods peer review, DO-178C), they are mid-bar. The two structural failures are: (1) the design-lock skill cannot enforce its own forcing functions because nothing mechanically gates the "Locked" status — the most recent locked design in `docs/Working/` (`field-never-set-diagnostic.md`, dated this evaluation's day) is missing every one of the sections the skill claims it refuses to lock without; and (2) the skill lacks any notion of formal semantics, falsifiability, or independent challenge — which is exactly the rigor PL/formal-methods review brings, and exactly what Precept's "compile-time structural checking" and "soundness over completeness" commitments demand of itself. The reviewer agent inherits both gaps and adds its own: it is structurally a checklist verifier, not an independent epistemic challenger.

The skills produce **defensible** design and review work. They do not produce work that would withstand a Rust subteam meeting, a TC39 stage advancement vote, or a POPL submission rebuttal phase.

## Findings

### F-1: The "refuses to lock" forcing functions don't bind — evidence in-tree
**Category:** STRUCTURAL
**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Comparison:** Rust RFC (where missing sections genuinely block merging via human FCP); TC39 (stage advancement is a vote against criteria).

**What:** SKILL.md says nine separate times that the skill "refuses to lock" without Philosophy Alignment, Language Design Grounding, Architecture Grounding, four-leg decisions, source citations with excerpts, etc. But the skill is a markdown file read by an LLM at session start — there is no mechanical gate. The most-recent locked design in the repo, `docs/Working/field-never-set-diagnostic.md` (status "Locked 2026-05-25"), has **zero** of the required sections: no `## Philosophy Alignment`, no `## Language Design Grounding`, no `## Architecture Grounding`, no `sources-consulted` frontmatter, no per-decision `Sources consulted` leg. It touches language surface (retires `Writable` keyword, renames to `editable`, restructures the modifier catalog) and pipeline architecture (new graph-analyzer sub-pass, new catalog property) — exactly the territory the skill says requires those sections. The skill's refusal is rhetorical, not structural.
**Why it matters:** The catalog-driven philosophy of Precept is "metadata is enforced by the compiler refusing to build, not by convention." The skill that gates the design layer should hold itself to the same standard. Right now it doesn't — it's a convention dressed in the language of enforcement, and the in-tree evidence shows the convention being bypassed on the day of evaluation. Every recommendation downstream of this finding (better sections, stronger forcing functions) is moot if the lock gate isn't mechanically enforceable.
**Evidence:** `docs/Working/field-never-set-diagnostic.md` lines 1-9 (frontmatter says "Locked 2026-05-25" with no sources-consulted); the H2 section list omits Philosophy/Language/Architecture Grounding entirely. SKILL.md lines 175-181 ("Behavioral guards") promise refusal that cannot actually be enforced.
**Recommendation:** Add a `lifecycle-2-lint` companion — a CLI/analyzer that scans `docs/Working/*.md` for `status: Locked` frontmatter and parses for required sections, four legs per decision, citation-with-excerpt patterns, and `sources-consulted ⊇ per-decision-citations`. Wire it as a pre-commit hook on changes touching `docs/Working/` and as a CI gate. The skill becomes the *intent* layer; the linter becomes the *enforcement* layer. Until that ships, downgrade the skill's prose from "refuses to lock" to "instructs the author to fill" — at least the doc would be honest.

### F-2: No formal semantics, no reduction rules, no soundness obligation
**Category:** MISSING
**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Comparison:** PL research peer review (POPL/PLDI/ICFP standard: small-step or big-step operational semantics, type rules, soundness theorem); Rust RFC for type-system features (reference-level explanation with desugaring).

**What:** The skill requires "state the evaluation semantics precisely — binding, evaluation order, type inference implications" (Language Design Grounding) and the reviewer requires "formally defensible semantics" with semantics that "can be stated precisely" (§ 11). But neither offers a notation, a template, or a worked example. There is no requirement for typing rules, no requirement for reduction rules, no requirement for stating what fact about the design must remain true under future extension (a soundness claim or stability invariant). For a language whose Principle 11 is "Static completeness — if a precept compiles without diagnostics, it does not fault at runtime" — a property that is literally a soundness theorem — the design lock skill has no place for the author to state the soundness obligation their feature introduces or preserves.
**Why it matters:** Precept's `docs/philosophy.md` and `precept-language-spec.md` § 0.1 commit to prevention, determinism, totality, and static completeness. Each is a formal property. A feature that touches expression evaluation, modifier semantics, or proof obligations either preserves these or weakens them. Without a forcing function to state the semantic-rule change, designers describe behavior in prose, and the question "does Principle 11 still hold after this feature ships?" goes unanswered until runtime breakage surfaces it. The proof-engine spec (§ 0.6) explicitly demands "soundness over completeness" — but no design doc in the tree carries a soundness statement.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md § Language Design Grounding (line 90) asks for "evaluation semantics precisely — binding, evaluation order, type inference implications" without any structural support — no inference-rule template, no reduction-rule template, no obligation to state what's preserved. `precept-language-spec.md` § 0.1 Principles 7, 10, 11 are soundness statements that no design doc in `docs/Working/` carries an obligation against. Compare TC39: every type-system-adjacent proposal carries a "Semantics" section with at minimum an evaluation algorithm; Rust RFC type-system proposals carry typing-judgment notation.
**Recommendation:** Add a required `## Semantic Rules` section, gated on the same trigger as Language Design Grounding. Require at minimum: (a) for new expression forms — a small-step reduction rule in prose-or-notation form ("E[set X to e] → E'[X' = v]"), (b) for new typing rules — a Hindley-Milner-style inference rule sketch with premises and conclusion, (c) for new proof obligations — what fact must the proof engine establish before the construct is accepted, (d) a soundness preservation claim: "Principle N continues to hold because …" naming the specific principle(s) the feature could threaten. The skill should refuse "Locked" if the design touches expression evaluation, proof obligations, or constraint semantics without a Semantic Rules section.

### F-3: Citation-with-excerpt is good — but defeatable, unidirectional, and uncited "no precedent" gets a free pass
**Category:** RIGOR
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** DO-178C derived requirements traceability (bidirectional — every requirement traces both up to a parent and down to verification artifacts); academic literature reviews (a survey must include rejected alternatives the reader expects).

**What:** The citation-with-excerpt rule is the skill's strongest single forcing function — it's hard to fake without opening the file. But three weaknesses are designed in:
1. **Unidirectional.** The author cites what supports the decision. There's no requirement to cite the *strongest opposing source* — the paper, the language, the catalog principle that argues against this choice. A determined skim-and-paste author cites a confirming sentence and ignores the contradicting one. PL surveys and Rust RFCs both require "Prior art" sections that include systems with *different* answers and explain why they were rejected.
2. **"No precedent — novel choice" is an honest answer but accepts no cost.** The skill says "or explicit 'no precedent — novel choice, accepting risk'" (line 132-133) and the quick reference says "Accept 'no precedent — novel' as honest answer; do not invent precedent" (line 220). But a novel choice in a domain integrity language is the exact moment that needs the most external grounding, not the least. There's no escalation — no requirement that "novel" decisions get a second reviewer, a delay, or a stronger philosophy check. TC39 routinely refuses to advance proposals that lack co-implementer commitment; Rust requires multiple subteam approvals for design with no prior art.
3. **No cross-citation requirement.** A design can cite ten Precept-internal sources and one external source and pass the "engages broader field" check, even when the external citation is a peripheral note. Compare academic peer review: a paper claiming a contribution must situate it among the strongest comparable systems, not the most convenient ones.

**Why it matters:** Precept's positioning ("no other tool provides this combination") and its philosophy ("honesty about approximation") both depend on the design layer being epistemically honest about what's actually different and why. Unidirectional citation is selection bias dressed as rigor.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md lines 130-141 (per-decision citation legs) — no "Strongest opposing source" leg, no "Contradicting precedent considered" leg. Lines 219-220 (Quick reference) — no escalation for "no precedent." Lines 89-98 (Language Design Grounding) — no requirement for a comparable-system citation that took a *different* answer.
**Recommendation:** Add a sixth per-decision leg: **"Strongest counter-evidence"** — the source (internal or external) that most plausibly argues against this decision, with excerpt, and a one-sentence response. Honest "no counter-evidence found after looking" is acceptable but must say where the author looked. Additionally, add a "novel decision escalation" rule: if any decision is "no precedent — novel choice," the skill marks the design `status: Locked-Novel` (distinct from `Locked`) and the reviewer agent treats Novel locks as requiring a second epistemic challenger pass before promotion to canonical.

### F-4: "Philosophy Alignment" can be box-ticked because the principles are catalog-shaped but the check is prose-shaped
**Category:** RIGOR
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** Bret Victor / Hillel Wayne "invariants stated up front"; DO-178C derived-requirements traceability.

**What:** The Philosophy Alignment section asks the author to "address each commitment specifically" via four prompts (prevention, approximation honesty, determinism/inspectability, compile-time checking). But: (a) philosophy.md actually carries more than four commitments — "one file, complete rules," "mandatory rationale," "governance not validation," "stateless precepts are first-class," "primary author is domain expert" are all canonical and load-bearing; the skill's prompts are a curated subset, not the full set; (b) each prompt is prose-shaped — "does this enforce at compile time" — which can be answered "yes" without proof; (c) the section asks "does it tension" — but tension assessment is exactly where designers under-detect their own conflicts (the cognitive bias the skill exists to counter). A box-ticking answer like "this enforces at compile time because the new diagnostic fires at compile time" passes the literal prompt while ignoring the deeper question.
**Why it matters:** Precept's identity is the philosophy — `CLAUDE.md` says "Every design and implementation decision is evaluated against these." A skill whose philosophy check is partial and prose-shaped under-protects the project's identity precisely at the point a designer is most likely to drift. The "domain expert is primary author" commitment, in particular, has direct language-design implications (terseness, readability, conceptual load) that the current four prompts don't surface.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md lines 73-83 — four prompts; philosophy.md § "What makes it different" carries five distinct commitments and § "The hierarchy of concepts" carries three more (data primary, state instrumental, stateless first-class). `precept-language-spec.md` § 0.1 enumerates eleven principles; the skill prompts cover four. The asymmetry is structural under-coverage.
**Recommendation:** Replace the four prose prompts with a **principle-coverage matrix**: every canonical principle from `precept-language-spec.md` § 0.1 (the eleven) is a row; each row has columns "Affected?" (Y/N), "How served" (1 sentence + cite), "Tension" (1 sentence or N/A), "Tradeoff" (1 sentence or N/A). The skill auto-populates the matrix from the spec at lock time (or fails honestly if the spec changes). This shifts philosophy-alignment from "remember to address each" to "fill the matrix." Designers cannot quietly skip a principle. Companion change: extract the canonical principle list into `docs/language/principles.yaml` (or a section of the spec marked machine-extractable) so the matrix is data-driven, not prose-driven — itself a catalog-system move that Precept's architecture demands.

### F-5: No designated "reviewer-of-the-design" independence — the reviewer agent shares the designer's framing
**Category:** PROCESS
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** DO-178C structural independence between developer and verifier; PL peer review (PC members who advocated cannot review).

**What:** The precept-reviewer agent reviews diffs and design docs. It has clear rules and reads CLAUDE.md. But it operates in the same session, with the same context, often invoked by the same author who just wrote the design. There is no structural separation between "the person who wrote the design" and "the person who must challenge it." The reviewer is presented findings checklist-style against the rules; it does not assemble an independent model of the problem and compare. § 13 (Source Verification) is the closest thing to independence — it requires the reviewer to open every cited source — but it stays inside the design's frame of reference.
**Why it matters:** The most common failure mode in design review is shared-blindspot — both designer and reviewer miss the same thing because they came in through the same door. Formal-methods venues structurally prevent this by assigning reviewers who didn't see the proposal during gestation. Precept's high-bar ambition is undermined by every design and review happening in one continuous reasoning thread. The reviewer also lacks any mandate to ask "what's the strongest argument *against* this design?" — that's the kind of question peer review trains people to ask and that the reviewer rules don't require.
**Evidence:** precept-reviewer.md lines 7-9 ("You are a critic, not a fixer") — but the critic role is checklist-checking, not independent-modeling. § 13 (lines 122-127) verifies cited sources but does not require the reviewer to assemble an independent claim about the problem and compare. No section instructs the reviewer to formulate a counter-design before reviewing. Compare a typical academic review: "Summarize the paper in your own words" and "What is the strongest objection a competent reviewer could raise" are standard prompts.
**Recommendation:** Add to the reviewer agent a mandatory **"Independent re-statement"** preamble before findings: the reviewer must, in 3-5 sentences, restate (in its own words) what the design is doing and why — *then* compare that restatement to the design's own framing. Mismatches between restatements are first-class findings (CONCERN: "Design frames this as a diagnostic; reviewer's restatement frames it as a catalog-property change with a diagnostic as a consequence — the catalog property is the larger surface and warrants its own discussion"). Also add a mandatory **"Strongest objection"** finding category: even on APPROVED designs, the reviewer names the strongest reason a future engineer might regret this — recorded as a NIT, intended for the postmortem-style retrospective if the design ages badly.

### F-6: No falsifiability / "what would prove me wrong" obligation
**Category:** MISSING
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** Bret Victor / Hillel Wayne "tools for thought" — name failure modes; Karl Popper falsifiability; experimental-science preregistration.

**What:** Acceptance criteria are test-shaped ("this passes / this fails as expected") — which is good for verifying the implementation does what the design says. But there is no place in the skill for the author to state **what evidence would falsify the design's premise** — what observation in production, what user feedback, what corpus pattern would force them to admit this design was wrong. The Tradeoff leg names known downsides, but a known tradeoff is precisely *not* a falsifier — it's an accepted cost. Falsifiers are the *unknown* downsides whose discovery should reverse the decision.
**Why it matters:** Precept's runtime hasn't shipped to external users. Many design decisions are betting on assumptions about how domain experts will reason, what error messages they need, which abstractions will scale. Without a falsifiability obligation, designs become unfalsifiable in practice — every observation gets reinterpreted to confirm the original decision. This is the failure mode that the philosophy commitment "honesty about approximation" implicitly opposes but the skill doesn't operationalize.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md acceptance criteria section (lines 148-150) is test-shaped, not falsifier-shaped. The "Tradeoff accepted" leg (line 131) is downside-stating, not falsifier-stating. Compare Hillel Wayne's "Are We Really Engineers?" — engineering disciplines that ship safety-critical work require explicit hazard analyses naming what evidence would force a recall.
**Recommendation:** Add a `## Falsifiers` section, required for any design that locks behavior visible to external authors (language surface, error messages, diagnostic codes, MCP vocabulary). Format: 2-5 specific observations that, if seen post-ship, would force a redesign. Examples: "If three or more samples in `samples/` need `# noqa: FieldNeverSet` annotations after the corpus sweep, the diagnostic is over-firing and severity should drop to Info" — concrete, measurable, decision-changing. Pair with `/lifecycle-7-audit` (periodic drift detection) so falsifiers are revisited rather than buried.

### F-7: Architecture Grounding stops at Precept-internal boundaries
**Category:** RIGOR
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** C++ ISO Evolution / Library / Core subgroup split; AWS PRFAQ "outside-in" framing.

**What:** Architecture Grounding asks layer placement (catalog vs pipeline vs API vs tooling), cross-component propagation (Runtime / Tooling / MCP), and breaking changes. Then it asks "What do comparable systems handle for this kind of design problem?" — a single open prompt at the end. But the framing is Precept-internal — what layer in Precept, what propagation across Precept components. There is no obligation to engage with how comparable compiler architectures (Roslyn's incremental model, CEL's type registry, OPA's compiler/builder split, CUE's lattice) solve the *architectural* problem the design touches, only the *language-surface* problem (which Language Design Grounding covers).
**Why it matters:** Precept's catalog-as-spec inversion is itself a strong architectural claim. New designs that touch the catalog-pipeline boundary are betting on the inversion holding for one more case. Without engagement with how Roslyn (descriptor + diagnostic split — already cited in `compiler-and-runtime-design.md`), CUE (lattice-based evaluation), or OPA (compiler vs builder boundary) handle similar boundaries, the design has no external pressure on its architectural reasoning. Internal consistency is necessary but not sufficient — the project's own `compiler-and-runtime-design.md` § 2 explicitly grounds the catalog-driven choice in CEL/OPA/CUE comparisons, and locked designs should hold themselves to the same comparative standard.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md lines 99-116 — three sub-prompts on Precept-internal layers + one "general architecture" prompt at the end. No requirement to cite a specific comparator. Contrast `docs/compiler-and-runtime-design.md` § 2.2 ("Catalog-driven design") which cites CEL/OPA/CUE/Dhall in support of every architectural claim.
**Recommendation:** Restructure Architecture Grounding into two halves — **Precept-internal placement** (current three sub-prompts: layer / propagation / breaking) and **External architectural precedent** (mandatory: cite at least one comparator's solution to the architectural problem the design touches, with excerpt, and explain the divergence). The reviewer agent treats a missing external comparator on a non-trivial architectural change as a CONCERN.

### F-8: No teachability / error-message / domain-expert-reasoning check
**Category:** MISSING
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** AWS PRFAQ ("imagine the press release"); user-research-driven product design; Bret Victor "tools for thought."

**What:** Precept's primary author is the domain expert ("not the developer" — `philosophy.md`, `CLAUDE.md` § Product Philosophy). The skill's prompts evaluate language design in terms of binding, evaluation order, type inference — engineer-shaped concerns. There is no prompt that asks: *will this design be teachable to the domain expert in 10 minutes? what's the error message a domain expert sees when they get this wrong? does this design make their reasoning easier or harder?* The single ambitious statement — "primary author is domain expert" — is structurally invisible in the design lock skill, which optimizes for compiler-architecture rigor without an equivalent "domain-expert ergonomics" rigor.
**Why it matters:** This is the single largest gap measured against Precept's own stated positioning. A language can be theoretically sound, catalog-driven, philosophically aligned — and still ship a feature that no domain expert can use because the error message is incomprehensible or the conceptual surface area exceeded what the audience can hold. The skill protects the language's *correctness* but not its *audience fit*. Every comparable user-facing product framework (AWS PRFAQ, Stripe's API design reviews, Apple's HIG critiques) requires articulating the experience from the audience's point of view.
**Evidence:** No section of SKILL.md asks about teachability, learnability, or the domain-expert experience. Acceptance criteria are test-shaped, not experience-shaped. Compare `philosophy.md` § "Who authors a precept" — domain expert / business analyst, not a developer — vs the skill's complete absence of any prompt about that audience.
**Recommendation:** Add a `## Audience and Teachability` section, required for any language-surface change. Required content: (a) the worked example a domain expert would write using this feature, in 5-10 lines of `.precept`, (b) the error message the domain expert sees when they get one specific thing wrong, with the specific aspect explained, (c) a one-paragraph "10-minute teaching path" — what the domain expert needs to read to use this feature, ordered. The reviewer treats a missing Audience section on a language-surface change as a BLOCKER. This is the single most leveraged change against Precept's stated identity and the easiest to operationalize.

### F-9: No reversibility / blast-radius / migration-cost analysis
**Category:** RIGOR
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** TC39 (stages are explicitly about commitment — Stage 3 means implementations exist and changes are costly); Rust RFC "Drawbacks" + "Future possibilities."

**What:** The Tradeoff leg names a downside. The Open Questions section forces resolution. But there is no obligation to assess *reversibility*: if this design ships and turns out wrong in six months, how hard is it to undo? Adjacent: no blast-radius assessment — how many corpus files, tests, docs, and third-party consumers would a reversal touch? The field-never-set-diagnostic design itself silently incurs a major reversibility cost (retiring `Writable` keyword, renaming `writable` → `editable` across the entire corpus and all docs) — and the skill structure does not surface that the cost-of-reversal is now higher than the cost-of-shipping-the-feature-as-is.
**Why it matters:** Precept's runtime hasn't shipped externally yet — reversibility is currently cheap, but every design that ships locks in a piece of public surface (catalog member names, keywords, diagnostic codes flow to MCP vocabulary, completions, hover, grammar). Without an explicit reversibility prompt, the skill makes it equally easy to lock a reversible change and an irreversible one. After consumers ship `.precept` files using these features, reversal cost becomes infinite.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md "Tradeoff accepted" leg (line 131) accepts known downside, doesn't measure reversal. No section asks "consumers post-ship." Contrast Rust RFC structure: every RFC has a "Future possibilities" section partly to surface "if we ship this, what becomes harder to ship later."
**Recommendation:** Add to each decision a sixth/seventh leg: **"Reversibility"** (Easy / Hard / Effectively-irreversible-post-ship, with one-sentence justification) and **"Blast radius"** (catalog members touched, docs touched, samples touched, external consumers affected). Aggregate at the top of the Decisions section as a "Lock cost summary." The skill refuses to lock a design with Effectively-irreversible reversibility unless the design also carries a Falsifiers section (F-6) — the two checks together force "if this could be wrong forever, name what would prove it wrong."

### F-10: "Source" is open category but no source-type taxonomy → unfetchable citations slip in
**Category:** RIGOR
**Severity:** MODERATE
**Comparison:** Academic citation standards; ISO documentation requirements.

**What:** The skill says "Source is an open category — anything with a permanent address" (line 138-140, line 192). This is a reasonable principle, but it lets external URLs slip into citations that the reviewer can't actually fetch (precept-reviewer.md line 199 acknowledges this: "If you couldn't open a cited source, say so explicitly… skipped — unfetchable"). An unfetchable citation is, in practice, an unfalsifiable claim wrapped in a citation veneer. The skill doesn't ask the author to mirror unfetchable sources (paste full excerpt with timestamp) or to favor in-tree citations over URL citations.
**Why it matters:** The citation-with-excerpt rule is the skill's strongest forcing function. URLs to external papers, blog posts, or vendor docs degrade that forcing function because the verifier can't reproduce the source. Reproducibility is the basis of independent verification — without it, the cite-and-excerpt rule becomes "trust the author copied the excerpt accurately."
**Evidence:** precept-reviewer.md line 199 — explicitly acknowledges the "unfetchable" gap and shifts the burden to a CONCERN against the design, but the SKILL.md doesn't prevent the citation in the first place. No requirement to mirror, archive, or prefer-local.
**Recommendation:** In SKILL.md, add: external URL citations must (a) include the full quoted excerpt verbatim (no paraphrasing or truncation), (b) include the access date in the citation, and (c) be preferred only when no in-tree or paper-PDF equivalent exists. For citations to standards (RFCs, ISO docs, papers), require a stable identifier (RFC#, DOI, paper title + venue + year) and prefer locally cached copies in `research/references/`. Add `lifecycle-2-design --archive-citations` mode that downloads/mirrors external URL citations as text snapshots, committed alongside the design doc.

### F-11: The skill is encoded in a markdown file the LLM may not read in long sessions — wrong encoding
**Category:** ENCODING
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** Mechanically-enforced systems (TypeScript strictness modes; Rust's `#![deny(...)]`); reviewer-as-process rather than reviewer-as-document.

**What:** SKILL.md and precept-reviewer.md are 229 and 207 lines respectively. They encode rules in prose meant to be loaded into an LLM's context. In long sessions, mid-task, with other context competing for attention, the LLM is not guaranteed to apply every rule. The "refuses to lock" guarantees are LLM-judgment guarantees, not code-enforced ones. F-1 shows the empirical failure mode — a Locked doc landed today without the required sections. This is a property of the *encoding*, not the rules. The rules are good. The encoding cannot enforce them.
**Why it matters:** The catalog system inside Precept exists precisely because Precept's own architects concluded that "rules encoded as code in pipeline stages drift and degrade — encode them as data the compiler enforces." The same architectural argument applies to the design-lock and review skills themselves. A skill encoded as markdown is the legacy "rules in pipeline stages" pattern at the meta-level.
**Evidence:** F-1 evidence applies here directly — `field-never-set-diagnostic.md` is the empirical proof that prose-rules-in-markdown don't enforce. Also: nothing in the skill's structure prevents a session from skipping it entirely (the trigger phrases must match user intent, the LLM must read the skill, the LLM must apply the rules — three independent failure points).
**Recommendation:** Split the skills into two artifacts: (a) `lifecycle-2-design.md` (the *intent* layer — read by humans and LLMs, owns the why and the prompt structure) and (b) a structured `lifecycle-2-schema.yaml` plus a `lifecycle-2-lint` analyzer (the *enforcement* layer — required-sections list, per-decision-leg validators, citation regex, frontmatter set-membership check). The analyzer runs as a pre-commit hook on `docs/Working/*.md` with `status: Locked` and as a CI gate. The LLM still helps the author *fill* the schema; the lint enforces *completeness*. Architecturally, this mirrors how Precept itself separates language definition (catalogs) from pipeline execution (generic machinery).

### F-12: No staged advancement model — Locked is binary, no "Draft → Stage 1 → Stage 2 → Locked"
**Category:** PROCESS
**Severity:** MAJOR
**Comparison:** TC39 (Stages 0-4 with explicit advancement criteria); Rust RFC (Open → Final Comment Period → Merged); IETF (Informational / Experimental / Standards-track).

**What:** SKILL.md treats the design lifecycle as draft → Locked. The status fields `Locked YYYY-MM-DD` and `Draft` are the only two states the frontmatter accommodates. There is no "Stage 1 — semantics drafted," "Stage 2 — external precedent surveyed," "Stage 3 — implementation prototype exists," "Stage 4 — locked." Without staged advancement, every design either lurks in Draft (which the in-tree evidence shows is fine even for nominally-locked designs) or jumps to Locked. There is no productive in-between where partial confidence accumulates.
**Why it matters:** Precept's own designs have variable confidence levels. A simple severity choice (Decision 2 in field-never-set) is genuinely low-risk; a keyword retirement (Decision 5) is high-risk. Treating both as a single Locked event under-weights the high-risk decision and over-bureaucratizes the low-risk one. TC39's staging exists precisely to match process weight to decision risk. Without staging, Precept gets a single Lock event for both — and the empirical evidence (F-1) is that the high-bar event happens *less* rigorously than the rules state, because the same effort floor applies to every decision.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md frontmatter template line 55 — `status: Locked YYYY-MM-DD`, no staged variants. No advancement criteria per stage anywhere in the skill. Compare TC39 — Stage 2 (Draft) "Initial spec text exists" vs Stage 3 (Candidate) "Spec text is complete… test262 acceptance tests written… implementation in two engines" — explicit, measurable, staged.
**Recommendation:** Replace binary Locked with four stages: `Draft` → `Semantics-Stated` (Decision text + Semantic Rules section per F-2 exist; no implementation required) → `Externally-Grounded` (Language Design Grounding + Architecture Grounding + external precedent citations complete) → `Locked` (all of the above + acceptance criteria + doc-update enumeration + falsifiers). Each stage has its own advancement criteria the skill enforces (or the linter from F-11 enforces). Designers can move at the speed appropriate to risk — small severity decisions go Draft → Locked in one session; keyword retirements ladder through all four.

### F-13: No security, observability, or evolvability dimension
**Category:** MISSING
**Severity:** MODERATE
**Comparison:** AWS Well-Architected Framework (security pillar); Bret Victor "make the system inspectable to itself"; OWASP for input-handling DSLs.

**What:** SKILL.md has no prompts for: security implications (does this construct expand the attack surface of `.precept` ingestion? does it enable resource-exhaustion or pathological-input attacks?), observability (when this construct misbehaves at runtime, how does the operator diagnose it? does it propagate to traces, logs, structured outcomes?), or evolvability (when the .NET ecosystem changes — NodaTime API, ICU, UCUM revision — what's the migration story?). Precept's lexer enforces a 64KB source ceiling "as a security guardrail" (`precept-language-spec.md` § 1) — security is a stated concern, but no design-level prompt operationalizes it.
**Why it matters:** Precept is positioned to govern medical claims, financial instruments, regulatory filings — domains where security, audit, and observability are first-class concerns. A skill that doesn't surface these dimensions silently bets that the designer happens to remember them. Compare AWS Well-Architected: every workload review must address Security, Reliability, Performance, Cost, Operational Excellence — explicit pillars precisely because designers reliably under-weight them under deadline.
**Evidence:** No section of SKILL.md mentions security, attack surface, observability, audit, traceability, telemetry, or migration. The lexer's 64KB ceiling is the only stated security control and it's spec-level, not design-process-level.
**Recommendation:** Add an optional `## Operational dimensions` section, required for any change touching: (a) source-text ingestion (lexer/parser) — security prompt about adversarial input; (b) runtime evaluation — observability prompt about how violations surface to operators; (c) external dependencies (NodaTime, ICU, UCUM, ISO 4217) — evolvability prompt about upstream changes. Each prompt is one paragraph; the skill auto-skips prompts that don't apply to the design's scope.

### F-14: The reviewer's "Source Verification" is the right idea but stops short of independent source-discovery
**Category:** PROCESS
**Severity:** MODERATE
**Comparison:** Peer-review "missing references" critique; DO-178C requirements completeness analysis.

**What:** precept-reviewer.md § 13 (line 122-127) requires the reviewer to open every cited source. It also encourages "look for uncited sources the design should have consulted" (line 126) — which is excellent — but treats this as a soft additional step rather than a structured deliverable. There is no required output like "uncited-sources-the-design-should-have-consulted" frontmatter, and no enumeration of source types the reviewer is required to actively check (e.g., for any catalog-touching decision: the reviewer should always open the relevant catalog file regardless of whether the design cited it).
**Why it matters:** The strongest part of independent review is the reviewer noticing what the designer didn't even know to look at. Making this discretionary ("look for uncited sources") under-uses the reviewer's epistemic position. The reviewer's value is exactly the source-discovery the designer didn't perform — formalizing it raises the floor.
**Evidence:** precept-reviewer.md line 126 — discretionary phrasing. Line 194 — `sources-uncited-but-checked` exists in the frontmatter template but the obligation is soft.
**Recommendation:** For specific design-change categories, make source-checking *mandatory* regardless of whether the design cited them:
- Catalog member change → reviewer always opens `src/Precept/Language/<Catalog>.cs` and the corresponding catalog doc.
- Diagnostic change → reviewer always opens `Diagnostics.cs` and `docs/compiler/diagnostic-system.md`.
- Modifier-keyword change → reviewer always opens `Modifiers.cs`, `TokenKind.cs`, `Tokens.cs`, `Lexer.cs`.
- Language-surface change → reviewer always opens `precept-language-spec.md` § 0.1 (the principle list) and runs the principle-coverage check (F-4).
The reviewer reports these as `sources-mandatorily-checked` in the frontmatter; missing entries are a process violation, not a finding against the design.

### F-15: "Decisions" section has no notion of decision *grain* — small and large decisions look identical
**Category:** RIGOR
**Severity:** MODERATE
**Comparison:** Software-architecture ADR practice; decision-cost-of-reversal frameworks.

**What:** The Decisions section requires four (now per F-3 / F-9, six or seven) legs per decision. But every decision gets the same treatment, regardless of stakes. The field-never-set doc's Decision 2 (severity = Warning, not Error) is a low-stakes recoverable choice; its Decision 5 (retire `Writable` keyword across the entire language) is a high-stakes near-irreversible choice. They get the same four-leg structure. The grain mismatch incentivizes either over-investing in low-stakes decisions or under-investing in high-stakes ones (and the empirical evidence — F-1 — is that under-investment wins).
**Why it matters:** Process weight should track stakes. Treating every decision identically is the bureaucratic failure mode — it makes the high-stakes decisions look like the low-stakes ones, hiding the actual risk profile of the design.
**Evidence:** SKILL.md lines 123-146 — single Decision template, no stakes/grain distinction. Field-never-set Decision 2 (~10 lines) and Decision 5 (~10 lines) get visually equal treatment despite vastly different stakes.
**Recommendation:** Decisions self-classify by stake: `stakes: low | medium | high | irreversible`. Low-stakes decisions need only Rationale + Tradeoff (two legs). High-stakes need all current legs plus Reversibility (F-9), Counter-evidence (F-3), Falsifiers (F-6). Irreversible requires all of the above plus a 24-hour cooling-off period (the design carries `status: Stage-3-Externally-Grounded` for at least 24 hours before advancing to `Locked`). The skill flags any design where any decision is stakes:irreversible and acts accordingly.

## What the skills do well

Three things are genuinely strong:

1. **Citation-with-excerpt rule.** Requiring a verbatim excerpt alongside the source identifier is the single strongest forcing function in the skill. It is unusual outside academic review and it is correctly placed. Despite F-3's critique of its unidirectionality, the underlying mechanic — "you cannot fabricate the excerpt without opening the source" — is exactly right. Keep this.

2. **Doc-update enumeration with auto-population from the routing table.** Pre-populating the doc-update section from `CLAUDE.md`'s routing table closes a real failure mode (designs that ship without doc updates because the author forgot). The mechanic is sound and well-suited to the catalog-driven architecture. This is the operational glue between the design-lock skill and the promote skill, and it works.

3. **Anti-mirror / category-error framing in the reviewer.** § 12 of precept-reviewer.md ("frame it as a category error — name what layer the behavior belongs in, what layer it is incorrectly placed in") is genuinely sophisticated review framing. It maps directly to Precept's catalog-vs-pipeline architectural distinction and forces reviewers to articulate the principle, not just the symptom. This is the right level of abstraction for a reviewer of a metadata-driven system.

## Final recommendation

If only **three** changes are made, in order:

1. **Build the `lifecycle-2-lint` analyzer (F-11) and gate `docs/Working/*.md` with `status: Locked` on it.** Without mechanical enforcement, every other improvement is rhetorical. The catalog-system architectural philosophy that built Precept demands this for its own design process — eat the dog food. This single change moves the skill from convention-of-courtesy to architectural-discipline.

2. **Add the `## Semantic Rules` section, required for any change touching expression evaluation, proof obligations, or typing rules (F-2).** This is the single largest gap measured against the project's stated commitments (Principles 7, 10, 11 are formal properties), and it is the gap that has the biggest impact on whether the language stays sound under future extension. A template with reduction-rule notation and a soundness-preservation claim is one section the project has to write *once* and benefit from on every feature.

3. **Add the `## Audience and Teachability` section, required for any language-surface change (F-8).** This is the single largest gap measured against the project's stated positioning ("primary author is the domain expert"). It's also the lowest-cost change — a worked example, a sample error message, a 10-minute teaching path — that directly counters the failure mode where catalog-driven correctness ships features no domain expert can use.

If **ten** changes can be made, add in priority order:

4. **Principle-coverage matrix for Philosophy Alignment (F-4).** Replaces prose prompts with a row-per-principle matrix derived from `precept-language-spec.md` § 0.1. Closes the box-ticking gap and forces engagement with the full principle list, not a curated subset.

5. **Staged advancement model (F-12).** Replace binary Draft/Locked with Draft → Semantics-Stated → Externally-Grounded → Locked, each with its own criteria. Allows process weight to track decision stakes; pairs with the linter from #1.

6. **Strongest-counter-evidence leg per decision (F-3).** Adds the sixth leg that forces the author to cite the source most plausibly arguing against the decision, with excerpt and response. Closes the unidirectional-citation gap.

7. **Reversibility + Blast-radius legs per decision (F-9).** Two additional legs (or one combined "Lock cost") per decision; "Irreversible" classification triggers cooling-off period and mandatory Falsifiers section.

8. **Independent re-statement preamble for the reviewer agent (F-5).** Forces reviewer to articulate the design in its own words before checking findings. Closes shared-blindspot failure mode.

9. **Falsifiers section, required for any external-author-visible change (F-6).** 2-5 observations that would force redesign post-ship. Pairs with `/lifecycle-7-audit` for revisit discipline.

10. **External architectural precedent requirement in Architecture Grounding (F-7).** Splits Architecture Grounding into Precept-internal placement + external comparator citation. Closes the inside-out-only critique.

The remaining findings (F-10, F-13, F-14, F-15) are MODERATE-severity refinements that should follow once the top-ten are in place — they raise the ceiling, but the floor is what's currently failing.
