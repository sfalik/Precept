# Prove vs. Defer: The Compile-Time/Runtime Boundary Across Verification Systems

> Neutral research brief. Six comparators, one subsection each. No design recommendation for Precept — synthesis at the end characterizes *patterns*, not a prescription.

## Framing: two different axes bundled under "prove vs. defer"

The comparator systems below actually split the question two ways, and conflating them muddies the picture:

1. **What can the tool's checker *decide at all* given the annotations it has** — i.e., what's inside its decidable/reasoning fragment vs. what it must reject as unprovable, ask for an annotation, or flag as an alarm.
2. **What happens to the property once the annotation gap is closed or the check times out** — does the system have a *runtime* fallback (contract monitoring, a rejected transaction), or is there no runtime concept at all (a specification method with no execution semantics until a separate code-generation/refinement step)?

Two of the six comparators (Event-B, and to a lesser extent Dafny/Whiley/SPARK-without-a-deployed-app) have **no runtime concept** — the "defer" side of the prompt's question doesn't apply to them the way it does to Eiffel or the abstract interpreters. That distinction is preserved explicitly per subsection below rather than papered over.

## 1. Refinement types — Liquid Haskell, F*

**Where the boundary sits.** Both systems reduce a "value satisfies constraint" obligation to an **SMT-decidable logical implication**, not to interval abstraction and not to concrete evaluation. Liquid Haskell's own framing is explicit about *why* the boundary is drawn where it is:

> "Liquid Haskell takes great care to ensure that type checking is decidable and efficient. To achieve this, it has to be careful to generate verification conditions that are decidable and efficiently checkable by the SMT solver."
> — Vazou, *Refinement Types* (lh-course Lecture 01), quoted at `research/architecture/compiler/interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:75-76`

The predicate grammar is restricted to "variables, booleans, numbers, boolean operators, equality, linear arithmetic, uninterp. functions" (ibid., line 77) — the decidable QF-UFLIA fragment. Anything outside that fragment is not a compile-time question the type system can answer; it simply isn't expressible as a refinement.

F* sits at the fused end of the same spectrum: its type-and-effect system computes weakest-precondition-style verification conditions **as part of typing itself**, dispatching them to Z3 in the same pass rather than in a separate downstream stage (`research/architecture/compiler/type-proof-stage-contract-survey.md:98-103`, flagged there as a Tertiary inference from the Dijkstra-monad framing rather than a verbatim architecture statement). F*'s own site: "combines the expressive power of dependent types with proof automation based on SMT solving and tactic-based interactive theorem proving" (ibid., line 100-101).

**What forces an obligation to runtime / to the author.** Two separate forcing functions:
- *Undecidable-fragment obligations* are forced onto the **author**, not to runtime: Liquid Haskell's canonical example is `{v:Int | v /= 0}` for a divisor — "the caller must establish the non-zero precondition or type-checking fails" (`interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:83`). This is a compile-time rejection with a directive, not a deferral.
- *Genuinely unprovable-by-the-tool-at-present* obligations are handled by an explicit escape hatch rather than silent pass-through. Liquid Haskell's termination checker allows `{-@ lazy foo @-}` to "assume termination" when a decreasing metric can't be found (`research/architecture/compiler/proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:322-323`), and `{-@ fail foo @-}` inverts the check (declares a definition *expected* to be unsafe; if it type-checks, that's itself an error) (ibid., lines 332-333). F* has the parallel `admit()` — "forces F* to accept the current goals as true (even if they are false)" (Max Taylor, "Using F* to Formally Verify Programs," maxtaylor.dev, 2023) — and an `assume` construct that injects an unproved hypothesis into the context, both understood by the F* community as deliberately **unsound escape hatches** for incremental development, not a defer-to-runtime mechanism (no separate runtime check ever fires for the admitted fact — the program simply compiles with an unverified claim baked in).

**Discharge mechanism.** Type ascription/refinement annotation on the binder + subtyping-as-implication-check: `{v:b | p₁} ⊆ {v:b | p₂}` holds "if p₁ 'implies' p₂ for all values v of type b… checked… based on the SMT solver" (`interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:80-81`). Nothing is evaluated; the check is a validity query over the refinement predicates.

**Soundness vs. completeness.** Both are **sound by construction within the decidable fragment** (an accepted program's refinements really do hold) and *incomplete outside it* — anything not expressible in the fragment is a compile error, not a false "safe." The escape hatches (`lazy`, `fail`, `admit`, `assume`) are the tools' own admission that the decidable core sometimes can't reach a true fact, and using them is understood by both communities as opting out of soundness for that one obligation, tracked and visible in the source (an explicit pragma/keyword at the call site), not a silent gap.

## 2. SMT-backed verifiers — Dafny

**Where the boundary sits.** Dafny translates a resolved/typed program to Boogie IR, which generates per-procedure verification conditions dispatched to Z3 (`type-proof-stage-contract-survey.md:47`, row "Dafny → Boogie"). It does not use interval abstraction at all — "Ranges over integers are expressed as formulas like `0 <= v && v < a.Length`. No interval widening is performed; the SMT solver reasons exactly over the formula" (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:391-393`). The load-bearing design choice is **modular, per-method verification**:

> "Dafny treats each method in isolation. When analyzing a method, Dafny 'forgets' the bodies of all called methods and only uses their pre/postconditions. This means the accuracy of verification depends entirely on the quality of the annotations."
> — `proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:395`

**What forces an obligation to the author (not runtime — Dafny has none until code generation).** Loop invariants are the sharpest example: "Without a loop invariant, Dafny cannot verify properties that depend on what the loop computes" (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:416`). The `requires`/`ensures`/`invariant`/`decreases` vocabulary is exactly the vocabulary the author must supply to make an obligation provable — absent it, Dafny reports a specific, attributed failure (`file.dfy:13:14: Error: postcondition might not hold`, `proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:437`), never a silent pass.

**Discharge mechanism.** Weakest-precondition calculus over the Boogie IR: assignment substitutes into the postcondition, sequential composition chains WPs, branches verify each arm separately (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:399-406`). Classification is exhaustive and named per outcome — Verified / postcondition-might-not-hold / assertion-might-not-hold / loop-invariant-might-not-hold / decreases-might-not-decrease / precondition-might-not-hold (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:422-432`); there is no silent "probably fine" bucket.

**Soundness vs. completeness — the escape hatch.** Dafny's `assume` statement is the documented, explicitly-unsound escape valve: "Sound verification requires that every assumption be justified in some way, but such justification lies outside the use of Boogie" (Boogie/Dafny community documentation, corroborated via `github.com/dafny-lang/dafny` issue discussion, 2026 access) — and the community convention of writing `assume false;` as a placeholder while a proof is unfinished is widely documented practice (Max Taylor and multiple Dafny tutorials, 2023–2026), functioning exactly like Liquid Haskell's `lazy`/`admit`: a visible, grep-able marker that a specific obligation was *not* discharged, rather than a silent gap. Because Dafny has no runtime contract-monitoring mode — the verified program compiles straight to C#/Java/Go/Python (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:450`) — an `assume` is the only place an unproven fact can enter the compiled artifact; there is no second-chance runtime check standing behind it.

## 3. Abstract interpretation — Astrée, Frama-C EVA

**Where the boundary sits.** Both reason over an **abstract state** (interval + congruence, with relational domains added in Astrée) propagated forward through the program, never over concrete values and never via SMT. The defining move, per Cousot's foundational framing quoted in the internal survey, is to "use that denotation to describe computations in another universe of abstract objects" and "accept incompleteness for soundness" (`interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:52-55`). Frama-C EVA is explicit that this yields a one-sided guarantee: **"EVA is sound for the absence of alarms: if no alarm is emitted for an operation, the operation is guaranteed not to cause a runtime error. Conversely, an alarm indicates a possible (not necessarily actual) runtime error"** (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:121`). Astrée's framing of the same idea, for a NIST-recognized sound analyzer: **"'Sound' means: if Astrée reports no error, the absence of that class of error has been formally proven"** (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:201`).

**What forces an obligation to runtime.** Nothing is literally "deferred to runtime" by these tools — they analyze C/C++ source without an accompanying runtime contract layer. The analogous event is the **alarm**: when the abstract value of a subexpression includes a value that would cause a runtime error (e.g. the abstract divisor set includes 0), an alarm fires (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:145`) rather than a hard compile rejection — the program still compiles and runs; the alarm is advisory. The forcing condition is precisely **loss of precision at a join or widening point**: branches split and rejoin the abstract state at merge points (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:142`), and loops require **widening** to guarantee termination, which "may be overly imprecise, leading to false alarms" (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:154`). The non-relational limit of the interval domain itself is a further forcing function: Miné's paper states plainly that a correctness argument depending on an invariant relating two variables "is beyond the scope of interval analysis" (`interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:62-63`) — this is why Astrée additionally composes relational domains (octagon, polyhedra) (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:222-224`).

**How authors discharge obligations.** Not via preconditions on a supplier/client contract (there is no such vocabulary here) but via **supplying external bound knowledge** to sharpen the abstraction: Astrée lets the user "fine-tune precision for individual loops or data structures… by supplying external knowledge (e.g., loop bounds)," and with tuning "Astrée can produce exactly zero false alarms on structured sequential embedded code" (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:240`). This is the alarm-suppression analog of Liquid Haskell's precondition or Dafny's invariant: the author feeds the analyzer a fact it can't derive on its own.

**Soundness vs. completeness / escape hatch.** These tools deliberately **over-reject relative to actual runtime behavior** (false alarms are the accepted cost of soundness) rather than ever *under*-reject. There is no "unsafe" escape hatch analogous to `admit`/`assume` in the verified-artifact sense — an alarm is a diagnostic, not a proof obligation the author can locally discharge by fiat; the closest analog is delta analysis (Astrée) that tracks which alarms are new vs. pre-existing across revisions (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:257`), letting a team triage rather than eliminate the noise.

## 4. SPARK / GNATprove

**Where the boundary sits.** SPARK layers **two distinct proof mechanisms** with an explicit ordering: a cheap, bound-propagation "Interval" pass runs first, and only what it can't settle escalates to SMT. The `gnatprove.out` summary table has a dedicated column for each: **"Interval — Proved by the lightweight bound-propagation interval pass"** vs. **"Provers — Proved by an SMT prover (CVC5, Z3, Trivial internal simplifier, or user-specified)"** (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:33-58`). The bound source is the **declared Ada subtype range**: "Ada's type system carries explicit range constraints (e.g., `type Score is range 0 .. 100`), which GNATprove uses as initial interval bounds" (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:37`) — this is the direct analog of "reject-unbounded-source" in `interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:47`.

**What forces an obligation to the caller.** SPARK's contract vocabulary states the forcing rule as directly as any comparator in this survey:

> "The precondition of a subprogram specifies constraints on callers of the subprogram." … "It may be necessary to add a precondition to the subprogram for analyzing its callers." … "The default precondition of `True` used by GNATprove when no explicit one is given may not be precise enough."
> — SPARK User's Guide, Subprogram Contracts, quoted at `interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:118-121`

The absence of an explicit precondition doesn't silently pass — it defaults to `True`, which is imprecise enough that callers typically fail their own proof obligations until the author supplies the real precondition.

**How authors discharge obligations.** Preconditions/postconditions/type invariants for the SMT tier; declared subtype ranges for the Interval tier; for collection cardinality specifically, membership is handled as a **discharged precondition, never a forward inference** — the formal-container `Insert` contract for a set is `Pre => Length < Capacity OR Contains(x)`, with `Contract_Cases` splitting on `Contains` to give the exact `+0`-or-`+1` semantics (`research/architecture/compiler/count-cardinality-bound-proof-survey.md:193-217`). This is the same "supply the fact that makes it provable" discipline extended from scalar ranges to container membership.

**Soundness vs. completeness / escape hatch.** SPARK's classification table has a named bucket precisely for the over-reject-vs-escape-hatch question: **"Justified — Not proved; user suppressed it with `pragma Annotate` (direct justification)"** vs. **"Unproved — Not proved and not justified"** (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:58`). `pragma Annotate` is the explicit, auditable escape hatch — a human justification attached at the source location, distinct from and never silently equivalent to an actual proof. Severity levels (`low`/`medium`/`high`) further triage unproved checks by estimated real-world likelihood (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:78`), and unproved checks report *why* — "provers reached time and step limit" or "provers gave up" (`proof-engine-interval-arithmetic-survey.md:86-87`) — rather than failing opaquely.

## 5. Event-B

**Where the boundary sits — and why "runtime" doesn't apply the same way.** Event-B has **no runtime concept as such**. It is a specification/refinement method: an abstract machine (state variables, invariants, guarded events) is refined stepwise toward code, with a **Proof Obligation Generator** producing a proof goal at each refinement step, discharged by automated or interactive theorem provers in the Rodin platform (`research/philosophy/formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:83-85`). The proof is a **deductive guarantee, not an operational one**: "Event-B generates a proof obligation for each event: given that the invariants hold before the event fires, prove they hold after… Event-B's guarantee is deductive (formal proof); Precept's [comparator, for contrast] is operational (runtime enforcement)" (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:168`).

**What forces an obligation to remain unproved / to the author.** When the automatic provers can't discharge a proof obligation, Rodin routes it to **interactive proving** — a human supplies the missing lemma or witness inside the Rodin tool. External corroboration: "By making use of feedback from unproved proof obligations during model construction, developers are guided towards construction of a model that has fewer errors and is more easily proved" (Abrial et al., "An Open Toolset for Modelling and Reasoning in Event-B," ICFEM/FMCO lineage, cited via search of eprints.soton.ac.uk, 2026 access — Secondary, not fetched verbatim due to access gating, so treated as a corroborating rather than sole load-bearing source). There is no "ship it anyway and catch it at runtime" option in the method itself — Event-B's refinement chain is not executed until a final refinement is translated to a programming language (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:89`), so an undischarged proof obligation blocks progress on that model, it does not degrade to a runtime check.

**How authors discharge obligations.** Refinement — starting from an abstract specification and formally refining it to a concrete data structure "while proving the refinement preserves all invariants" (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:170`) — plus explicit guards on each event (an event may only fire when its guard holds) and invariants restated/strengthened at each refinement level.

**Soundness vs. completeness / escape hatch.** Event-B's posture is closer to "no escape hatch, no partial credit" than any other comparator here: an obligation is either discharged (by automatic or interactive proof) or it remains open, and remaining open is a visible, tracked state in the Rodin proof-obligation dashboard rather than a silent default. This is possible precisely *because* there is no runtime fallback pulling weight — the method's entire value proposition is that the abstract machine cannot reach an invalid state **by proof**, so incompleteness in the tooling shows up as unfinished work on the model, not as a residual runtime risk.

## 6. Design-by-contract — Eiffel

**Where the boundary sits.** Eiffel is the sharpest contrast to the other five: **contracts are explicitly not compile-time checked.** "Contracts in Eiffel are not just wishful thinking. They can be monitored at run time under the control of compilation options" (eiffel.org, "ET: Design by Contract, Assertions and Exceptions," official docs, accessed 2026-07-11). The entire prove-vs-defer question is answered by **deferring to a configurable runtime monitor** rather than by any static reasoning fragment at all.

**What forces (and controls) the runtime check.** A compilation-option-controlled monitoring level, escalating in scope:

> `no`: assertions have no runtime effect; `require`: monitor preconditions only; `ensure`: preconditions and postconditions; `invariant`: includes class invariants; `all`: includes check instructions and loop constructs.
> — eiffel.org, "ET: Design by Contract, Assertions and Exceptions," accessed 2026-07-11

The stated rationale for why this is a legitimate design (not a soundness failure) is explicit: **"for a correct system — one without bugs — assertions will always hold, so the compilation option makes no difference to the semantics of the system"** (ibid.) — i.e., the contract-checking is understood as *diagnostic instrumentation over a program the author already believes is correct*, not the mechanism that makes it correct. This is the polar opposite framing from Astrée/EVA/SPARK, where the static check *is* the safety mechanism.

**How authors discharge obligations, and the attribution split.** Preconditions state what "the routine is imposing on its clients"; postconditions state "what the routine guaranteed to its clients for calls satisfying the precondition"; class invariants are "implicitly added to both the precondition and postcondition of every exported routine" (all: archive.eiffel.com, "Design by Contract and Assertions," accessed 2026-07-11). Violation is attributed by contract role, not by generic failure: **"Precondition violation: signals a bug in the client, which did not observe its part of the deal. Postcondition or invariant violation: signals a bug in the supplier — the routine — which did not do its job"** (eiffel.org, ibid.) — a fault-attribution discipline none of the other five comparators state as explicitly (SPARK's severity levels come closest, but attribute *likelihood*, not *whose* bug it is). Under inheritance, the discharge obligation is further constrained by the **subcontracting rule**: a redefined routine "may keep or weaken the precondition; it may keep or strengthen the postcondition. Strengthening the precondition, or weakening the postcondition, would be a case of 'dishonest subcontracting'" (Meyer's rule, corroborated across eiffel.com/se.inf.ethz.ch sources, accessed 2026-07-11) — ensuring dynamic dispatch can never surprise a caller who validated against the base contract.

**Soundness vs. completeness / escape hatch.** The `no`/`require`/`ensure`/`invariant`/`all` monitoring dial *is* the escape hatch, applied wholesale rather than per-obligation: production code commonly ships with contracts disabled (`no` or a reduced level) for performance, trusting that pre-release testing with `all` caught violations. This means Eiffel accepts **no compile-time soundness guarantee whatsoever** — the entire guarantee is empirical (tested-and-monitored), and the "does it over-reject" question doesn't arise because nothing is rejected ahead of execution; a violation surfaces only on the concrete input that triggers it, as a runtime exception with "a precise message and a call trace" (archive.eiffel.com, ibid.).

## Synthesis: patterns in where systems draw the prove/defer line

Six systems, three structurally distinct postures on the same axis:

**Pattern A — Sound-but-incomplete static reasoning over a restricted fragment, with an explicit "supply the missing fact" discipline.** Liquid Haskell, F* (within its SMT-checkable core), Dafny, Astrée/EVA, and SPARK all share this shape: each defines (explicitly or by construction) a decidable/tractable reasoning fragment, reasons over an **abstraction of the value space** (a refinement predicate, a symbolic WP formula, or a numeric interval) rather than executing anything, and treats anything outside that fragment as *the author's problem to name* — a precondition, a loop invariant, an external bound, a `pragma Annotate` justification. What differs is *how* the fragment is bounded: Liquid Haskell/F* bound it by SMT-decidable logic (QF-UFLIA and beyond, at the cost of an opaque solver in the loop); Astrée/EVA bound it by the interval/congruence/relational abstract domain (cheaper, sound, provably blind to certain relational facts per Miné's own statement); SPARK layers both, cheapest first. **All five give an explicit escape hatch that is visible and auditable rather than silent**: Liquid Haskell's `lazy`/`fail`, F*'s `admit`/`assume`, Dafny's `assume`, SPARK's `pragma Annotate` (Justified bucket). None of these escape hatches is a "runtime fallback" in the operational sense — they are all *compile-time acknowledgments that a specific obligation was not discharged*, carried as a marker in the source, not a promise that something will catch the problem later.

**Pattern B — No runtime concept at all; an obligation that can't be proved simply blocks, and interactive proof (a human) is the fallback, not execution.** Event-B is the clean instance: the entire method exists upstream of any running system, so "defer to runtime" isn't an available move — the fallback for an undischarged automatic proof is interactive discharge inside the tool, and a model with open obligations is, by the method's own logic, an unfinished model, not a shipped one with residual risk.

**Pattern C — No static reasoning fragment; the entire guarantee is deferred to a runtime monitor that is itself optional.** Eiffel is the clean instance on the opposite pole: nothing is proved ahead of time, and the boundary is drawn not between "provable" and "not provable" but between "instrumented" and "not instrumented," with the instrumentation level a build/deployment knob. The correctness argument rests on the belief that a tested, contract-monitored program has no bugs left to catch — an empirical, not a deductive, claim.

**What forces the split, across all six.** Two recurring forcing functions explain *why* an obligation lands on one side of the line rather than the other, independent of which system:

1. **Expressiveness of the reasoning fragment vs. inspectability/determinism of the checker.** Every system that stays inside a bounded fragment (interval, octagon, QF-UFLIA) buys predictable, terminating, legible checking at the cost of provable blind spots (Miné's "beyond the scope of interval analysis," the QF-UFLIA ceiling, SPARK's Interval-then-SMT staging). Every system that accepts a fuller SMT/interactive-prover fragment (Dafny, Event-B's interactive tier, F*'s tactic layer) buys more expressiveness at the cost of solver opacity, non-termination risk, or human effort.
2. **Whether the checker's failure has anywhere to go.** Systems with a runtime home for a failed check (Eiffel's monitored contracts; implicitly, any system with a governed runtime backstop) can afford to be more conservative/incomplete at compile time, because an unprovable-but-actually-safe case is caught later rather than lost. Systems with no such home (Event-B's proof-gated refinement, Dafny's direct-to-target-language compilation with no monitored mode) must either prove it, get a human to discharge it interactively, or accept an explicit unsound escape hatch (`assume false;`) whose risk is entirely on the author's head.

These two forcing functions are largely orthogonal to each other in the survey: Astrée and SPARK sit at "narrow fragment, no runtime home, but the alarm/Unproved-bucket is visible and the fragment is chosen precisely to keep false alarms rare in practice"; Eiffel sits at "no fragment at all, full runtime home"; Event-B sits at "arbitrarily rich fragment (interactive proof can express anything provable), no runtime home at all." No comparator in this set combines "rich, effectively unbounded reasoning fragment" with "no runtime home and no escape hatch" — that combination would mean every unprovable-but-true fact blocks forever with no recourse, which none of the six systems accepts in practice.
