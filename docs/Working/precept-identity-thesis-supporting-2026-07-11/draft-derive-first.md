# A Second Opinion: What Precept Should Be and What It Should Guarantee

*Derive-first thesis, authored 2026-07-11. Forward-looking: every position below is argued from Precept's identity, audience, and value — never from "the code already does X." All internal citations verified first-hand against source at the cited lines. Agent-authored "locked"/"ruling" labels in internal docs are treated as arguments, not owner authority. The quarantined ruling cluster was not read.*

## 1. What Precept SHOULD Be

**Precept should be the single authoritative artifact of record for what a business entity's data is allowed to become — simultaneously a specification, a proof subject, and the production enforcement engine.**

The canon already names the identity: "a domain integrity engine for .NET — a single declarative contract that governs how a business entity's data evolves under business rules across its lifecycle, making invalid configurations structurally impossible" (`docs/philosophy.md:7`), where "the unifying principle is governed integrity — the entity's data satisfies its declared rules at every moment" (`docs/philosophy.md:9`). The guarantee object is the **configuration** — "(current lifecycle position, current field values), or for stateless entities, simply the current field values" (`docs/philosophy.md:43`, restated at `precept-language-spec.md:134,152`). Data and rules are primary; "state is instrumental, not primary. It is the coordinate system" (`docs/philosophy.md:37`); states are optional and stateless precepts first-class (`docs/philosophy.md:39`).

I endorse that identity, and I want to sharpen what it *entails*, because the entailments — not the description — settle every downstream boundary question:

**1a. Precept should be the fused artifact, not a pipeline of separate ones.** Precept's own comparator research concedes the model is not novel: "The mapping is exact. A Precept definition is an Event-B abstract machine in .NET DSL form" (`research/philosophy/formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:164`). Event-B proves the machine and stops; Eiffel enforces contracts at runtime and proves nothing. What Precept *should* be is the third thing neither is: one file that is at once the proved-sound definition, the deployed enforcer, and the live inspection API. Everything distinctive about Precept follows from refusing to split those roles (developed in §4).

**1b. Precept should be authored by domain experts, and that is a design constraint, not a marketing line.** "The primary author of a `.precept` file is a domain expert or a business analyst… not primarily software developers" (`docs/philosophy.md:92`). Taken seriously, this constrains the guarantee machinery more than any technical consideration: every compile-time claim must be statable in one teachable sentence, every rejection must name the missing fact in domain vocabulary, and any checking posture whose boundary a domain expert cannot predict is disqualified *regardless of its catch economics*. This audience commitment is why the spec is right to reject opaque solvers "even when they could prove more" (`precept-language-spec.md:225`) — and it will be the deciding vote on the prove-vs-govern boundary in §2.

**1c. Precept should be a governance architecture, not a better validator.** The category distinction is enforcement architecture, not rule expressiveness: "Invoked, not structural… The validator runs when called. If no code path calls it, the rules do not apply" versus "Structural, not invoked… No code path bypasses enforcement" (`research/language/references/governance-vs-validation.md:23,32-35`), closing four documented failure modes — Bypass, Timing Gap, Scattered Rules, Silent Mutation (`governance-vs-validation.md:131-183`). The forward implication: Precept's value never rests on checking *more things* than FluentValidation; it rests on there being **no unchecked path**. That guarantee is delivered by the runtime, which means the runtime governance leg is identity-constitutive, not a fallback.

**1d. Precept should keep the deliberately narrow execution model as a strategic asset.** No loops, no reconverging flow, closed type vocabulary, finite state space, purity, one file (`precept-language-spec.md:158-172`) — "These properties are the reason the language can support tractable compile-time proofs… The absence of widening is a feature" (`precept-language-spec.md:174`). The expressiveness price of proof has already been paid at the language-design level. A forward-looking thesis must either collect on that investment or explain why not (taken up in question (c) below).

**1e. Scope: one entity.** Precept governs integrity *within* a single entity type; referential integrity across entities is out of scope by design (`research/philosophy/domain-integrity-formal-concept.md:237`). This is the right boundary — it matches the ownership boundary of the entity — and it should be defended, not eroded.

## 2. What Guarantees It SHOULD Provide — the Prove-vs-Govern Boundary Derived

### 2.1 The two derivation axes

Every guarantee question below reduces to two axes, both derivable from identity:

**Axis 1 — Does a failed check have a graceful runtime home?** The evaluator's own outcome taxonomy splits failures into a **fault** — "a mid-evaluation abort with no recoverable typed outcome" — versus a recoverable governed refusal produced by working-copy discard (`research/architecture/compiler/fault-floor-definition-2026-06-11.md:122`; same split independently derived at `research/architecture/compiler/compile-time-guarantee-boundary-2026-06-10.md:39-42`). A failure with no graceful home cannot be "governed"; there is nothing to govern, only an abort. A failure with a graceful home (a typed refusal carrying its mandatory `because`) is *exactly what governance is for*.

**Axis 2 — Is the fact decidable from the definition alone, and could any runtime input repair it?** (`compile-time-guarantee-boundary:39`). Whole-definition facts (reachability, contradiction, sufficiency of carried constraints) are facts no single-instance execution can compute; concrete-value facts (does *this* claim amount satisfy *this* rule) are facts no compiler can know.

Crossing the axes yields four dispositions, and I will argue each is forced by identity, not preference:

| Disposition | What lands here | Posture |
|---|---|---|
| **PROVE (reject if unprovable)** | Evaluation-fault freedom — the fault floor | Prove-or-reject, total, no deferral |
| **PROVE (reject on proven violation)** | Definition incoherence — self-contradicting definitions | Always-Error; a third category, neither fault nor flag |
| **FLAG (warn on proven violation only)** | Universal definition-quality facts beyond the floor | Flag-sound warnings; silence promises nothing |
| **GOVERN (enforce at runtime)** | Concrete-value satisfaction of every declared constraint | Ingress + commit-time atomic enforcement |

### 2.2 PROVE, no deferral: the fault floor

Faults — division by zero, √negative, reading an unset optional, empty-collection access, out-of-bounds index, absent key, cross-qualifier arithmetic, representability overflow, temporal overflow, computed-arg computability (the eleven families enumerated at `fault-floor-definition:124`) — must be proven absent at compile time or the definition rejected. Three identity-derived reasons, none of them "the code does this today":

1. **Axis 1 forces it.** A fault fires mid-plan before any constraint verdict exists; the only runtime "handling" is an abort trap. Principle 10 commits to totality — "Runtime fault traps exist only as defensive redundancy for paths the compiler has already proven unreachable" (`precept-language-spec.md:110`). A product whose slogan is prevention cannot ship a failure mode whose only mode of appearance is an unrecoverable abort.

2. **Inspection honesty forces it.** Full inspectability is a non-negotiable principle (`precept-language-spec.md:98`), and the designed authoring loop evaluates the full machinery per keystroke. For a live "Certain" verdict to be honest, fault-freedom must already be proven — otherwise the inspection itself can crash and the verdict is a lie; and "Possible" must denote only awaiting-external-input, never "the engine declined to analyze" (`compile-time-guarantee-boundary:66-72`). This gives the floor a precise stopping rule I fully endorse: *the proof engine must reach exactly far enough that the only thing left unknown to live inspection is genuinely-external input not yet supplied* (`compile-time-guarantee-boundary:72`).

3. **The composition seam makes prove-or-reject compatible with external input.** The compiler "proves a structural fact — that the value carries its constraint — never the value itself; the runtime enforcement is what makes that carried constraint true, not a second line of defense" (`docs/philosophy.md:55`; `precept-language-spec.md:270`). This is the load-bearing invention: the proof's precondition is discharged by governance, so nothing is left to a runtime check and nothing external blocks the proof. This "supply the fact that makes it provable" discipline is the mainstream posture of every serious verifier — Liquid Haskell's `{v:Int | v /= 0}` divisor ("the caller must establish the non-zero precondition or type-checking fails," `research/architecture/compiler/interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art-2026-06-05.md:83`), SPARK's supplied preconditions (`interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art:118-121`) — with Precept's twist that the "caller" obligation is discharged automatically by its own ingress governance rather than by a human at every call site.

**Two consequences the floor's own logic demands, stated forward:** (i) **position-totality is part of the claim** — an obligation must exist at *every* fault-prone expression position, or Principle 11's sentence ("If a precept compiles without diagnostics, it does not fault at runtime," `precept-language-spec.md:112`) is false; the research documents ten live fail-open positions today (`fault-floor-definition:126,140`), which is drift between an implemented-flavored spec claim and reality and must be closed, not annotated. (ii) **Constraint discharge must be write-aware and actually enforced** — a standing constraint may discharge a fault obligation only if no unaccounted same-transaction write can falsify it, and only if the discharging constraint is genuinely enforced at every ingress/commit lane (`fault-floor-definition:128`); a proof whose precondition governance does not actually make true is not a proof, it is the seam of `philosophy.md:55` quietly severed.

### 2.3 PROVE on proven violation: definition incoherence

A definition whose own declared defaults violate its own declared rules, or whose initial state is unsatisfiable, is broken in a way *no runtime input can ever repair* — Axis 2's pure case (`compile-time-guarantee-boundary:48`). This should stay an always-Error rejection, and it should be recognized as a **third category** — "proven self-contradictions where no valid Create exists" — distinct from both faults and warnings (`fault-floor-definition:132`). Note this category structurally requires *value*-level reasoning, not just intervals: an interval domain cannot represent `A == B`, string/boolean rules, or three-field sums, and the concrete default-fold catches seven of eight broken-default shapes that bounds-only reasoning would pass clean (`research/architecture/compiler/bounds-only-constraint-enforcement-2026-06-05.md:40-47,64-79,147-152`). The two mechanisms are complementary, not competing (`bounds-only:142`) — a forward design should keep both and say so plainly.

### 2.4 GOVERN: concrete-value satisfaction — including declared bands

Every declared constraint is enforced at runtime on every value entering through the contract, atomically, on a working copy discarded on failure (`precept-language-spec.md:268`; `precept-language-spec.md:149`). This is prevention — the invalid configuration never exists — and it is the half of the guarantee that closes the four validator failure modes. Nothing here is second-class: for a product whose category claim is governance-not-validation (`precept-language-spec.md:140-142`), the runtime leg *is* the headline.

**The contested cell, settled from identity: declared-band containment belongs here, not in the fault lane.** The spec currently asserts the opposite in three places, and I am obligated to quote and propose rather than rewrite:

- §0.7:266: fault prevention includes "…no empty-collection access, **no result outside a declared bound**."
- Principle 11 (`:112`): fault classes include "…**constraint range impossibility**…"
- §0.6 item 6 (`:208`): "An assignment expression provably outside the target field's constraint range is a compile-time error."

Derivation: constraint modifiers are, by the spec's own locked definition, **rule shorthand** — "`min 5` and `rule X >= 5` are interchangeable to the proof engine. Proof participation is a function of a constraint's decidability, not its syntactic form" (`precept-language-spec.md:1138`, §2.4:1135). A rule violation at runtime is a recoverable, governed refusal — the value computes, the working copy is discarded, a typed `ConstraintsFailed` carries the mandatory rationale. By Axis 1, that is governance's home turf, not a fault. Treating band containment as prove-or-reject creates the *twin-syntax incoherence* the identity cannot tolerate: the same business meaning written as a modifier vs. a rule receives different guarantee levels (`docs/Working/compile-time-niche-decision-packet-2026-06-10.md:184`, probe-verified), which violates §2.4's interchangeability promise directly. It also over-rejects on merely-unknown intervals and forces bound-invention — a ceiling like `max '10000000.00 USD'` on an open-ended claim amount is "representability masquerading as policy" and anti-honest by Principle 8's own lights (`niche-packet:129`; corpus: 214 unbounded money fields vs 10 bounded, `bounds-only:120-122`).

**With one principled exception, derived from the composition seam:** a declared bound that *discharges a fault obligation* is proof-carrying — the fault proof rests on it — so writes into such a field must either prove preservation or reject (`fault-floor-definition:132`). The boundary is proof-carrying vs. not, never band vs. not. This preserves prove-or-reject exactly where the fault guarantee needs it and returns everything else to governance.

**Owner-gated recommendation A (spec):** amend §0.7:266's "no result outside a declared bound" clause, Principle 11's "constraint range impossibility," and §0.6 item 6 to a proven-violation + governance-enforcement reading, landing as one coordinated move with the runtime wiring that makes bands enforced at ingress/sweep — never an intermediate state where bounds are enforced nowhere (per the coordination constraint at `fault-floor-definition:203-206`). I reach this independently of any internal ruling: it falls out of §2.4 interchangeability + Axis 1 + Principle 8.

### 2.5 FLAG on proven violation: the universal-quality layer

Beyond the floor there is a real family of defects that *nothing else can catch*: dead reject rows whose failure signature is a clean wrong success; contradictory or vacuous rules (a refusal that should have happened and didn't — an absence per-scenario simulation cannot render in principle); guard-locked strands defeating "no workflow trapped in a state it can never leave" (`docs/philosophy.md:49`); clock-decay traps invisible to an inspector that evaluates at real wall-clock; decision-band gaps (`niche-packet:23-69`). The spec already commits to most of this — §0.6 items 7–12 are declared obligations, currently specification-only (`precept-language-spec.md:209-214,239-247`). Identity says these must ship: prevention that leaves an authored prohibition silently inert is a governance-integrity breach, and an auditor reading dead text is precisely the failure the mandatory-rationale principle exists to prevent.

But identity *equally* says they must ship as **flags under a flag-soundness contract, not as prove-or-reject**: "a flag is always a true universal statement about the definition; silence beyond the fault floor promises nothing" (`niche-packet:117`). Three derived reasons:

1. **The fragment boundary is invisible to the primary author.** The same unsatisfiable relation written three ways gets three different guarantee levels; parameterizing a constant silently exits the proven fragment (`niche-packet:184`). A domain expert cannot see a "single field vs. literal" boundary; a rejection posture over an invisible boundary fails the audience commitment (§1b).
2. **Prove-or-reject beyond the floor makes compiler upgrades breaking events** — every fragment extension retroactively flips previously-clean definitions to rejected (`niche-packet:117,184`). Under flag-soundness, engine growth only adds true statements.
3. **Proven-violations-only is already the locked proof philosophy** — "Flagging possible violations turns the compiler into a nag… when it speaks, it is right" (`precept-language-spec.md:223`). The flag layer is that philosophy applied above the floor.

Precondition, non-negotiable: the flags must actually be flag-sound before any identity claim attaches. A live probe showed PRE0155 firing a *false* "no valid configuration" claim on a satisfiable idiomatic tiered policy (`niche-packet:117,171`). One false universal claim does more damage than a hundred missing ones — it breaks Precept's most valuable diagnostic property, "when it speaks, it is right."

### 2.6 Separate lane, named honestly

Definition-evolution safety (a tightened rule stranding a persisted fleet, both versions compiling clean) is the most severe uncovered family and is structurally a *two-definition* deploy-gate problem, orthogonal to everything above (`niche-packet:106-108`). It should be scheduled as its own decision before first external users — folding it into either the floor or the flag layer would blur the teachable line.

### 2.7 The three open questions, reasoned out

**(a) Should MORE be pushed into compile-time proof — and is that practical?**

Split the question by the two things "more" can mean.

*More coverage of the existing claim*: unambiguously yes, and it is the highest-priority proof work that exists. Position-totality, the missing representability family, write-aware discharge, and the false-'Proved' repairs are not expansions — they are the difference between Principle 11's sentence being true and being false. This is completing a promise, not extending one.

*More rejection posture beyond the floor*: no — and this is where I land against the maximalist reading despite finding its evidence genuinely strong. The strongest case for all-in compile-time (silent wrong-success classes structurally mute to both governance and inspection; three inspector-blind defects shipped in hand-polished samples; DMN gap/overlap checking validated on real credit-lending tables — `niche-packet:139-156`) is a case for the *flag layer existing*, not for a rejection posture. The practicality ceiling is threefold: (i) the single most business-severe defect class — wrong-but-in-band formulas and constants — "is undecidable for ANY compile-time system; the live inspector… is the only catch that exists in any tool" (`niche-packet:90-92`), so proof expansion asymptotes well short of the defects that most matter; (ii) the tractable fragment (§0.4-enabled intervals + difference-bounds) is *provably* blind to equality, disjunction, three-variable sums, and non-numeric relations (`bounds-only:40-47`) — Miné's own statement that relational correctness arguments are "beyond the scope of interval analysis" (`interval-vs-value-evaluation-prior-art:62-63`) — and closing that with SMT is forbidden by the legibility commitment (`precept-language-spec.md:225`), correctly so for this audience; (iii) rejection-posture ceremony (bound-invention) lands on every author on day one and manufactures exposure to the evolution defect class nothing covers (`niche-packet:129,167`). The forward formula: **maximal proof coverage of the fault floor and incoherence; maximal flag-sound universal analysis above it; zero rejection-posture expansion beyond proof-carrying constraints.**

**(b) Does partial compile-time guarantee create false security — and what is the right response?**

The failure mode is real but specific. The gradual-typing/verification literature converges on this: partial static coverage misleads in one configuration — **when the boundary is illegible** — and the mitigations that measurably work are structural, not textual: per-obligation queryable disposition (SPARK's named assurance levels; Sorbet's `T.reveal_type`; Dafny's greppable `assert`/`assume` split), a check that materializes exactly at the frontier where static proof stops (Bader/Aldrich/Tanter, VMCAI 2018), and claims narrow enough to be falsifiable — while one-time prose caveats and repeated disclaimers are cosmetic (R2 §4; Lee & See 2004's calibration/resolution/specificity frame). Even a fully *sound* system misleads when its diagnostic attribution mismatches the reader's causal model (Tunnell Wilson et al., DLS 2018).

Precept's answer should therefore be **legibility first, and it is already constitutionally equipped for it** — this is the striking thing. Principle 4 already extends inspectability to "proven ranges, source attribution, and *what the engine could not prove*" (`precept-language-spec.md:98`); §0.6 #6 already mandates structured proof attribution "flowing as structured data, not parsed prose" (`precept-language-spec.md:231`); §0.6 #5's tri-state (proved dangerous / proved safe / unresolved) is exactly the per-obligation disposition the literature validates (`precept-language-spec.md:229`). The forward move is to *surface* this as a first-class per-rule guarantee disposition on the model — "this constraint: proven / governed-at-runtime / flagged" — queryable at the rule, projected into hover, docs, and the inspector. Precept can be the rare system whose guarantee boundary is a *product feature* rather than a footnote.

Two harder edges of honesty follow. First, **a lying ledger is the only genuine false-security machine here**: three verified false-'Proved' sites (`niche-packet:117,182`) are categorically worse than absent proof and outrank all breadth work. Second, the philosophy itself carries the exact "marketing-register absolutism beside a precise technical claim" configuration the literature warns about. `docs/philosophy.md:49` opens: *"Prevention, not detection. No errors. No bugs. Business logic cannot produce a wrong answer."* — while `:53-57` states the precise, correct version (prove-or-reject faults; carried constraints; "A definition that compiles without diagnostics has no unproven evaluation faults, no unreachable business process states, and no structural dead ends"). "No bugs" is falsified by the product's own honest concession that wrong-but-in-band values are undecidable. **Owner-gated recommendation B (philosophy):** temper `:49`'s absolute sentences to the scope `:57` already states — e.g., "No governed operation can produce a configuration that violates a declared rule; no compiled definition can fault or strand" — keeping the force, dropping the overclaim. Public copy must likewise never collapse the two mechanisms into "the compiler proves your data valid" (the Principle 8 overclaim named at `compile-time-guarantee-boundary:90`).

The response is emphatically *not* "more proof" (the ceiling in (a) means chasing completeness cannot close the gap) and not narrower claims alone (an accurate claim nobody can check per-site still miscalibrates). It is: sound ledger → legible per-obligation boundary → precisely-scoped claims, in that priority order.

**(c) Would relaxing everything to runtime governance leave Precept without a unique enough value proposition?**

Not *nothing* — but it would forfeit the defensible part, and the honest accounting has three lines.

*What survives full relaxation:* the governance architecture itself. Structural, lifecycle-aware, one-file, inspectable enforcement with no bypass path is genuinely absent from the landscape — the five-lane map has no other occupant of the "embeddable entity governance with lifecycle-aware data enforcement" cell (`research/product/entity-governance-landscape.md:416-430`), and the four failure modes it closes are real and cross-ecosystem (`governance-vs-validation.md:131-183`). A pure-governance Precept is still a differentiated product.

*What dies:* three things, each identity-constitutive. First, the **process half of the headline guarantee is compile-time by construction** — reachability, dead-end freedom, required-state dominance are whole-definition facts no runtime instance can compute (`docs/philosophy.md:51`; `compile-time-guarantee-boundary:47`); relaxation deletes "no workflow trapped in a state it can never leave" outright, not merely weakens it. Second, **inspection honesty collapses**: without the fault floor, a per-keystroke "Certain" verdict can crash or lie, and the inspector — the product's central authoring surface and the only catch for the most business-severe defect class — degrades from a trustworthy oracle to a best-effort preview. Third, **the language has already paid for proof**: the no-loops/closed-vocabulary/one-file austerity of §0.4 (`precept-language-spec.md:158-174`) is the price of tractable solver-free proof; a runtime-only Precept keeps the expressiveness bill and returns the purchase.

*The precedent line:* the nearest historical analog to "runtime-enforced, entity-bound, structurally non-bypassable contracts" is design-by-contract — Eiffel, and .NET's own Code Contracts — and both failed commercially in exactly this posture, with Eiffel's guarantee explicitly empirical (contracts a monitoring dial commonly disabled in production; "for a correct system… the compilation option makes no difference," eiffel.org, per R1/R3). That does not prove runtime-contract products can't win — the failure may have been ergonomics, not concept — but it does mean a runtime-only Precept walks into a category with a documented corpse in this jurisdiction and without the one property (a proved-sound definition + honest static contract) that distinguishes it from a revived Code Contracts with a lifecycle model. Conclusion: **the fault floor + incoherence rejection + flag-sound universal layer is the minimum proof leg the identity requires; below it, Precept is a better validator with a state chart — a real product, but a much smaller claim and a precedented graveyard.**

## 3. Where It Fits — the Category Map and the Owned Seam

| Lane | Representatives | What they own | What is structurally absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure validators | FluentValidation, Zod, JSON Schema, Bean Validation | Expressive shape/rule vocabulary, ecosystem integration | Structural enforcement (invoked, bypassable — `governance-vs-validation.md:43,64`); no lifecycle |
| State machines | Stateless, XState, Spring SM | Transition legality | No declared field model or data enforcement (`docs/philosophy.md:69`) |
| Rules/decision engines & BRMS | Drools, NRules, DMN | Decision logic over presented facts; business-user authorship, redeploy-free rule change | No entity binding, mutation, or lifecycle (`docs/philosophy.md:70`) |
| Policy engines | OPA, Cedar | Decoupled allow/deny at request time; hot reload | Enforcement decoupled — the caller must honor it; no entity/lifecycle (`docs/philosophy.md:73`) |
| Design-by-contract | Eiffel, .NET Code Contracts | Entity-bound, inherited, structurally-checked invariants | Runtime-monitor-only (Eiffel dial); no lifecycle model; commercially dead in .NET |
| Formal-methods tools | Event-B, TLA+, Alloy, Dafny | Design-time proof of the model | No runtime artifact, no deployment; specialist audience (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:139-142`) |
| Enterprise platforms | Salesforce, ServiceNow, Guidewire | The full combination — as a platform | Not embeddable; buyers pay for the platform, not the primitive (`docs/philosophy.md:74`) |
| DB constraints | CHECK/triggers | Structural enforcement at the storage layer | No lifecycle, no inspectability, no app-layer tooling (`governance-vs-validation.md:107-111`) |

**The seam Precept owns:** *entity-bound + lifecycle-aware + structurally non-bypassable + embeddable + definition-proved + inspectable*, in one artifact. It is the intersection cell, and the landscape research is right that no incumbent occupies it (`entity-governance-landscape.md:430`).

Four honest caveats a forward thesis must carry rather than bury: (i) the landscape memo is company-commissioned positioning research, the strongest form of an argument its author was asked to build — its "empty cell" is credible but not third-party neutral; (ii) the DbC lane shows the *concept* of structural entity-bound enforcement was tried in .NET and discontinued — the "empty cell" is empty at the lifecycle-integrated-DSL level, not the concept level, and the philosophy's comparator table currently omits DbC entirely (`docs/philosophy.md:65-77` lists ten comparators; Eiffel appears nowhere) — **owner-gated recommendation C:** add a DbC row that engages the precedent honestly; (iii) "domain integrity engine" is a category-creation play requiring buyer education, with a "why not a feature bolt-on to FluentValidation/XState?" objection the corpus never engages (`entity-governance-landscape.md:482` and R3); (iv) the BRMS buyer values (non-developer authorship *with* redeploy-free rule change) sit in tension with a compiled source-controlled `.precept` file — the domain-expert-author commitment (`docs/philosophy.md:92`) answers the authorship half but not the deployment half; this is a live product question, not a settled one.

Complements, not competitors: workflow orchestrators route, Precept constrains; event sourcing stores, Precept validates before the command is sourced; the ORM persists, Precept governs before the write (`docs/philosophy.md:80-84`). That framing is correct and should be kept aggressively — every one of those tools is a channel, not a rival.

## 4. What Makes It Unique — Engaging "Event-B Is Structurally Identical"

The finding is true and should be embraced, not deflected: "The mapping is exact… Same model, different derivation path, different deployment target" (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:164`). State variables/invariants/guarded events is the correct minimal model for invariant-governed state evolution; Abrial got there first. Claiming novelty of the *model* would be false. The defensible differentiator is elsewhere, and it is threefold:

**4a. The fused deployment target — occupying the cell no comparator occupies.** Across the verification landscape, every system picks one of three postures: sound-but-incomplete static checking with author-supplied facts and *no* runtime home (SPARK, Dafny, Liquid Haskell — an unproved fact enters production only through an explicit unsound escape hatch); proof-gated refinement with no runtime concept at all (Event-B — an undischarged obligation blocks the model, and "Event-B's guarantee is deductive… Precept's is operational," `formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:168`); or a runtime monitor with no static fragment (Eiffel — the entire guarantee empirical and switchable). **No surveyed system combines a prove-or-reject static floor with a governed runtime that is not a fallback but the *discharge mechanism of the proof itself*** (R1 synthesis). That is Precept's composition seam: "proven by the compiler, its precondition discharged by governance, nothing left to a runtime check" (`precept-language-spec.md:270`). In Eiffel the runtime check is a second line of defense one can switch off; in Precept it is the first and only line, load-bearing for the proof, and constitutionally non-optional. In Event-B the proof exists and then a human re-implements the machine; in Precept the proved artifact *is* the deployed machine — no refinement gap for divergence to enter. That fusion, not the abstract machine, is the invention.

**4b. Legible proof for a non-specialist author.** Every comparator that proves anything targets engineers or formal-methods specialists; Event-B "requires formal methods training… Not enterprise application developers" (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:91`), let alone business analysts. Precept's rejection of opaque solvers as a matter of principle (`precept-language-spec.md:225`), mandatory structured proof attribution (`:231`), tri-state truth classification (`:229`), and mandatory authored rationale on every constraint (`:108`) constitute a proof system *designed to be read by the person who owns the business rule*. Nobody else has built solver-free, deterministic, attribution-complete proof for a domain-expert audience — the niche packet is right that no external comparator even exists for that posture (`niche-packet:133` caveat 5), which is simultaneously the opportunity and the unmeasured risk.

**4c. Inspection as a runtime capability of the deployed artifact.** `Inspect` — a non-mutating, complete, per-event outcome preview available in production against the live instance (`docs/philosophy.md:18,60`) — has no analog in any lane: Event-B's ProB animator is a development tool, not a production API (`formal-spec-languages-comparators.md:178`); validators and DB constraints can only be probed by attempting the operation (`governance-vs-validation.md:108`). Combined with 4b, the unique promise is that the *reasoning* — compile-time and runtime — is a queryable surface, end to end.

The one-sentence differentiator, for a technical audience: *Precept is the Event-B abstract machine shipped as its own production runtime — with the proof's preconditions discharged by the runtime's own governance, and the whole reasoning chain, static and live, exposed as structured data legible to the rule's owner.*

## 5. The Value Proposition

For the buyer, three concentric claims, each independently defensible and each mapped to a mechanism:

**The replacement claim (entry point):** replace Stateless + FluentValidation + the glue code between them with one `.precept` file in which the lifecycle and the data rules are co-defined and the glue disappears (`entity-governance-landscape.md:450-463`). The pain is documented and convergent — lifecycle-conditional validation is hand-rolled in every ecosystem (FluentValidation `When()`, Bean Validation groups, XState's unconstrained `assign` — R3 §3), and the 10:1 FluentValidation:Stateless download ratio locates the funnel (`entity-governance-landscape.md:434-446`).

**The governance claim (the category):** not "we check thoroughly" but "the invalid configuration cannot exist" (`governance-vs-validation.md:195`). Every rule enforced on every operation, atomically, with a mandatory stated reason, with no code path that bypasses the contract — closing Bypass, Timing Gap, Scattered Rules, and Silent Mutation structurally. One artifact is simultaneously the enforcement, the documentation, and the audit trail: a `.precept` file is a rules register an auditor can read, in which — uniquely — a dead rule is a *compiler-reported* defect rather than dead text.

**The proof claim (the moat):** the definition itself is proven sound before any entity exists. Honestly scoped, the teachable sentence is: **"If your precept compiles, no operation can ever fault; anything the compiler flags is proven wrong for every possible entity; and everything your actual data decides is enforced on every operation, with a stated reason"** (the shape at `niche-packet:19`, which I independently endorse as the sentence the derived §2 boundary makes true — and which is true only after the floor's coverage gaps and the false-'Proved' sites are closed, `niche-packet:133` caveat 4).

And the meta-proposition that binds them, which is ultimately what §2's whole architecture is for: **Precept sells calibrated trust.** Its category is crowded with tools that either claim less than they could (validators) or more than they deliver (platforms, and every "compiles clean" gradient the false-security literature catalogs). A product whose per-rule guarantee disposition — proven, governed, or flagged — is itself a queryable, structured, honest surface is offering something none of the adjacent lanes can: not just enforcement, but a guarantee you can *interrogate*. That is the product the philosophy's principles, taken all the way forward, actually describe.
