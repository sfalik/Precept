FINDINGS — Adversarial review of the draft thesis, false-security lens

**1. The core mitigation ("per-rule disposition") is asserted as sufficient without addressing the primary overtrust vector R2 itself names: readers generalize a global signal onto every site.**

Passage: §2.7(b) — *"Precept's answer should therefore be legibility first, and it is already constitutionally equipped for it — this is the striking thing"* — followed by citing `precept-language-spec.md:98,231,229` as existing spec text, then proposing *"surface this as a first-class per-rule guarantee disposition on the model… queryable at the rule, projected into hover, docs, and the inspector."*

Gap: R2 §4.1 point 1 identifies the dominant overtrust mechanism as *"a single global claim standing in for a per-site fact"* — the reader sees "compiles clean" (the binary the compile step actually surfaces: 0 diagnostics vs N) and generalizes it across every constraint, without drilling into a secondary disposition panel. The draft's own target audience is explicitly *less* technical than the software engineers Tunnell Wilson et al. studied — who *still* misjudged even fully-sound diagnostics (R2 §1.6, §4.1) — yet the draft proposes a three-way tag (proven/governed/flagged) that a domain expert must proactively query, with no argument for why they would, when the ambient signal ("no errors") already answers a simpler, wrong question to their satisfaction. The passage treats the *existence* of a spec clause describing this capability as equivalent to the capability *working on the actual audience*, which is exactly the gap between "documented" and "measured" that R2 §4.2–4.3 distinguishes (structural mechanisms that are shown to work vs. cosmetic ones that are merely asserted).

What would make it honest: either (a) demonstrate the disposition surfaces *at the point of authorship* without requiring a query — e.g., every constraint's disposition is rendered inline next to the constraint itself, not behind a hover/query action, given the audience cannot be assumed to know to ask — or (b) concede this is an untested design hypothesis, not a "striking" solved problem, and route it to `/design` with the false-security risk named as an open question rather than a "constitutionally equipped" resolution.

**2. The endorsed "teachable sentence" bundles three different guarantee tiers into one flowing claim — the precise configuration R2 flags as producing overgeneralization.**

Passage: §5 — *"'If your precept compiles, no operation can ever fault; anything the compiler flags is proven wrong for every possible entity; and everything your actual data decides is enforced on every operation, with a stated reason' (the shape at `niche-packet:19`, which I independently endorse as the sentence the derived §2 boundary makes true…)."*

Gap: This sentence is offered as the buyer-facing proof claim — i.e., marketing register — yet it concatenates PROVE (universal, static), FLAG (universal-but-partial, static), and GOVERN (per-instance, dynamic, contingent on data actually flowing through the enforced path) with no visual or rhetorical break between them. R2 §4.1(1) and the TypeScript case study (§4.3: "opens by validating the complaint, then pivots to reassurance") both show that when a strong absolute clause ("can never fault") sits beside a conditional one ("enforced… with a stated reason") in the same breath, readers retain the strong clause and drop the qualifier on the weaker one. The draft cites this exact literature elsewhere (§2.7b) but never stress-tests its own endorsed marketing sentence against it — a self-application the "load-bearing claim verification" standard this repo otherwise holds itself to (per `.squad/skills/load-bearing-claim-verification/`, visible in the git status) would require.

What would make it honest: either split the sentence into three visibly distinct clauses with different visual/rhetorical weight (mirroring SPARK's named, distinct assurance levels rather than one continuous sentence), or explicitly flag in the thesis that this sentence is a *risk*, not a resolution, and that its bundling needs testing before adoption as public copy.

**3. Recommendation A reclassifies declared bands from PROVE to GOVERN — the exact syntactic surface (`min`/`max`) most likely to read as a compile-time type constraint to a non-specialist — without walking through the resulting authoring-time legibility change.**

Passage: §2.4, Owner-gated recommendation A — moves `min`/`max`/constraint-range clauses from Principle 11's fault classification and §0.6 item 6's "compile-time error" framing to proven-violation + runtime-governance, "excepting proof-carrying constraints." §2.4 itself concedes: *"a domain expert cannot see a 'single field vs. literal' boundary"* (echoing `niche-packet:184`) in the adjacent FLAG-layer argument (§2.5.1), but does not apply that same concession to bands.

Gap: Bounds are the single constraint shape domain-expert authors are most likely to mentally model as "the compiler enforces this" (they read like Ada range types or SQL CHECK, both of which the draft's own comparator table, §3, files under "structural enforcement at the storage layer" without noting SQL CHECK is *runtime*, not compile-time, adding to the confusion this reclassification could produce). The thesis derives the reclassification correctly from Axis 1/§2.4 interchangeability logic, but a reclassification that *moves a whole constraint family from a stronger to a weaker tier* is precisely the kind of change the false-security literature says needs its own legibility argument (R2 §4.2: "the check materializes exactly at the frontier where static proof stops… A boundary off by even one position… reopens the gap"). The draft treats this as a closed derivation (§2.4) and never returns to it under the false-security question in §2.7(b), even though it is the single largest concrete boundary-shift the thesis proposes.

What would make it honest: work the `min 5` example through the disposition-tag mechanism proposed in Finding 1 — show what a domain expert sees, before and after recommendation A, when they write `min 5` on an unbounded field, and confirm the tag communicates "this is enforced only when data actually flows through governed ingress, not proven absent of any possible violation."

**4. The FLAG layer produces a diagnostic signal indistinguishable from "nothing to check" at the exact granularity the gradual-verification literature says matters — no per-expression marker is proposed, only a coarser per-rule one.**

Passage: §2.5 (flag-soundness contract, *"silence beyond the fault floor promises nothing"*) combined with §2.7(a)'s own concession: *"the single most business-severe defect class — wrong-but-in-band formulas and constants — 'is undecidable for ANY compile-time system; the live inspector… is the only catch that exists in any tool'"* (`niche-packet:90-92`).

Gap: A definition containing an undecidable wrong-but-in-band constant produces the same "0 diagnostics" surface as a definition with none. This is structurally identical to `Any`/`any` propagation in mypy/TypeScript (R2 §1.3, §3 table, §4.1 point 2: *"nothing marks the boundary at the downstream use site once an `any` value has propagated"*). R2's own countervailing structural fix — Bader/Aldrich/Tanter's `?` marker living *"inside the specification itself, at the exact clause that is unproven, not a blanket file-level or project-level flag"* — is granularity below what the thesis proposes (a per-*rule* disposition, not a per-*expression*/per-*literal* one). The thesis's only answer for this specific defect class is the live inspector, which requires the author to manually enumerate every scenario — nothing marks, at the point of writing the literal, that it sits outside every analytic fragment and is inspector-only.

What would make it honest: either propose a per-expression/per-literal marker for values that fall outside every decidable fragment (a Dafny-`assume`-style, greppable, "unverified constant" annotation at the exact site), or explicitly name this as an accepted, unclosed residual risk in §2.7(b) rather than folding it into the general "legibility first" reassurance — right now it reads as covered by the same paragraph that also covers the (differently-shaped) fault floor and band cases.

**5. The fault floor's own soundness is admitted to be contingent on runtime-governance completeness — an implementation-integrity fact no per-rule static disposition tag can represent — and this is disclosed *after*, not instead of, a reassurance framing that structurally mirrors the pattern R2 names as ineffective.**

Passage: §2.2 consequence (ii): *"a proof whose precondition governance does not actually make true is not a proof, it is the seam of `philosophy.md:55` quietly severed"* and the "ten live fail-open positions" + "three verified false-'Proved' sites" admissions in §2.2 and §2.7(b). These appear *after* §2.7(b)'s opening claim that Precept "is already constitutionally equipped" for legibility and "this is the striking thing."

Gap: R2 §1.2 explicitly diagnoses this ordering as ineffective — Vanderkam's TypeScript unsoundness article *"opens by validating the complaint, then pivots to reassurance… treats the seven sources as things to learn to route around rather than a boundary the tool itself displays per-site."* The draft's §2.7(b) does the same move: reassurance ("already equipped," "striking") first, then the harder disclosure ("a lying ledger… three verified false-'Proved' sites… outrank all breadth work") second, in a subordinate paragraph. Substantively, the point stands (the draft does disclose it, unlike Vanderkam), but the *rhetorical structure* — which is exactly what the "false sense of security" question is about, since the thesis itself is the artifact an owner or downstream reader will form trust from — reproduces the pattern R2 catalogs as cosmetic-not-structural. More importantly: a per-rule "proven" tag cannot, by construction, represent whether the *proof engine's own coverage* of that tag is complete (fail-open positions, false-Proved sites are implementation bugs in the tagging mechanism itself, not facts about the specific rule). No amount of per-rule surfacing closes a bug in the surfacing mechanism.

What would make it honest: state plainly, in the same paragraph as the "constitutionally equipped" claim (not two sections later), that the legibility mechanism is only as trustworthy as the proof engine's own current completeness, that current completeness has three confirmed false-positive sites and ten fail-open positions, and that shipping the disposition tag *before* those are closed would itself be a false-security vector — a self-referential caveat the draft's structure currently defers rather than co-locates.
