# Witness/certificate checking as a soundness architecture for Precept's proof engine

> Precept's proof engine must never emit a *false proof* — claim a rule statically enforced when a runtime fault could still fire (spec §0.6 #1). Today a per-obligation `Proved` verdict is **trusted, not checked**: the engine computes a disposition and the `[StaticallyPreventable]` attribute *asserts* a `FaultCode`↔`DiagnosticCode` correspondence, but nothing re-verifies that the verdict is justified. This survey evaluates the **certifying-algorithm / witness-checking** architecture — an untrusted prover emits a witness that a *small trusted checker* re-verifies — as the soundness mechanism, **neutrally**, against two hard requirements (R1: solver-free + legible witness, §0.6 #3; R2: per-keystroke checking cost, ~44 ms corpus / ~3 ms worst-file baseline), and against the honest alternatives (verify the whole engine à la Verasco; heavy testing). Intended consumer: a `/design` on the proof engine's soundness architecture.

## Background

Spec-first check (the §0.6 grep). The canonical proof philosophy **decides** the *witness must exist and be legible* but does **not** decide *who checks it*:

- §0.6 #1 [Primary, in-repo]: *"The proof layer must never claim an expression is safe when it is not. Every proof path must return a provably correct result or conservatively decline."* — the soundness obligation, stated as a language-level commitment.
- §0.6 #3 [Primary, in-repo]: *"Proof witnesses must be structured data, not opaque solver traces … This is why SMT/Z3 solvers are excluded even when they could prove more: opaque proof witnesses violate the inspectability commitment."* — R1.
- §0.6 #6 [Primary, in-repo]: *"Every proven range must carry its source attribution … Proof results flow as structured data, not parsed prose."* — attribution exists.

The gap the spec does **not** close: §0.6 mandates a witness *for the author and tooling* (legibility/attribution), but nowhere mandates that the witness be *re-verified by a trusted checker* to catch a prover bug. The engine's `ProofObligation.Disposition ∈ {Proved, Unresolved}` (`proof-engine.md` §5) is the prover's *self-report*. The soundness of "Proved" rests on the correctness of the strategy code that produced it — a ~400–600-LOC, 10-strategy engine (`proof-engine.md` §4) that is **trusted in its entirety**. The one structural soundness link in the runtime direction is a *correspondence assertion*, not a check (`src/Precept/Language/FaultCode.cs`):

```csharp
[StaticallyPreventable(DiagnosticCode.DivisionByZero)]
DivisionByZero = 1,
```

The Roslyn analyzers PRECEPT0001/0002 enforce that *every evaluator failure path routes through a classified `FaultCode`* (`proof-engine.md` §"Proof/Fault Chain" invariant 2) — i.e. **routing completeness**, not **proof soundness**. Nothing checks that when the engine marks `DivisionByZero`'s obligation `Proved`, the divisor is genuinely non-zero on every reachable path. A bug in `ProofEngine.Intervals.cs` that mis-narrows an interval would emit a silent false proof — exactly the "soundness lie the engine must not tell" (`docs/Working/precept-guarantee-specification-2026-06-05.md` framing). This is the motivating gap.

This survey **builds forward** from `solver-free-static-analysis-techniques-survey.md` (the abstract domains Precept would use, and *the witness each naturally produces* — Karr equality basis, Farkas combination, congruence-closure explanation, DBM derivation chain) and treats those witness forms as the inputs to a checker. It does **not** re-survey the abstract domains themselves.

## Methodology

- **Research question.** Can a small trusted checker that re-verifies the proof engine's per-obligation witnesses enforce soundness (no false proof) under R1 (legible, solver-free witness) and R2 (per-keystroke checking cost), and how does the resulting trusted base compare to the alternatives of (a) formally verifying the whole engine and (b) heavy testing? Does the trusted base genuinely *shrink to a small kernel*, or does it merely *move*?
- **Comparator enumeration (not convenience-sampled).** Enumerated from the soundness-architecture literature spanning four tactics: **(A) certifying algorithms** (witness + simple checker — the foundational frame: Mehlhorn/McConnell et al.); **(B) proof-carrying code** (producer ships proof, consumer's validator re-checks: Necula/Lee); **(C) LCF / de Bruijn criterion** (small trusted kernel re-checks every proof: HOL/Coq/Isabelle); **(D) certified vs validated abstract interpretation** — verify-the-whole-analyzer (Verasco/CompCert) **vs** a-posteriori result validation (the closest analogue, since Precept *is* a small abstract interpreter); plus the certificate-format evidence from **SAT/SMT proof checking** (DRAT; Alethe/veriT) and **translation validation** (Pnueli) for the per-run-vs-per-tool axis.
- **Per-comparator sub-questions.** For each: (1) how is soundness achieved? (2) what is the trusted-base size? (3) checker complexity / per-run checking cost (R2)? (4) witness form and legibility (R1)? (5) applicability to a *small, finite, loop-free* abstract interpreter like Precept.
- **Search strategy.** Primary-source fetch (PDF → `pdftotext`) of: McConnell/Mehlhorn/Näher/Schweitzer *Certifying Algorithms* (Computer Science Review 2011); Jourdan/Laporte/Blazy/Leroy/Pichardie *Verasco* (POPL 2015); Wetzler/Heule/Hunt *DRAT format and DRAT-trim* (2016); Barbosa/Blanchette/Fleury/Fontaine *Alethe* (arXiv 2107.02354). LCF/de Bruijn-criterion and Pnueli translation-validation grounded from training-data knowledge (Tertiary — flagged in Threats); Necula PCC reached only via failed fetches, so the PCC claims are restated from the certifying-algorithm/Verasco evidence plus training-data (Tertiary, flagged). Precept-side state read directly from `docs/compiler/proof-engine.md`, `docs/runtime/fault-system.md`, `src/Precept/Language/FaultCode.cs`, and §0.6.
- **Inclusion / exclusion.** Included: soundness mechanisms where a *re-verification* step (by a checker, kernel, or validator) is the trust anchor. Excluded: the abstract-domain capability question (covered by the sibling survey) and SMT-as-prover (R1 fail — but SMT *proof certificates* are in scope as a witness-format data point).
- **Source-grade mix.** Primary: Certifying Algorithms (CSR'11), Verasco (POPL'15), DRAT (2016), Alethe (arXiv'21), Precept canon. Tertiary (flagged): LCF/de Bruijn criterion, Necula PCC specifics, Pnueli translation validation — reached via training-data, not fetched primary.
- **Time bounds.** External fetches 2026-06-06; Precept-side reads against working-tree HEAD on `spike/Precept-V2-Radical`, 2026-06-06.

## Findings

### The frame: certifying algorithms (the foundational theory)

A certifying algorithm emits, with each output, a witness that a *separate, simpler* checker verifies. This is the exact shape Precept's gap calls for [Primary; McConnell, Mehlhorn, Näher, Schweitzer, *Certifying Algorithms*, Computer Science Review 5(2):119–161, 2011, DOI 10.1016/j.cosrev.2010.09.009; access 2026-06-06; mirrored to `research/references/witness-checking/certifying-algorithms-mcconnell-mehlhorn-naher-schweitzer-csr2011.txt`]:

> "A certifying algorithm is an algorithm that produces, with each output, a certificate or witness … that the particular output has not been compromised by a bug. By inspecting the witness, the user can convince himself that the output is correct, or … this can be automated with a checker, which is an algorithm for verifying that w proves that y is a correct output for x." (§1)

The trusted-base shrink is the central claimed benefit — and it is *conditional* on the checker being both simple **and verifiable**:

> "In may [sic] cases, the checker is so simple that a trusted implementation of it can be produced, perhaps even in a different language where the semantics are fully specified. A formal proof of correctness of the implementation of the certifying algorithm may be out of reach, however, a formal proof of the correctness of the checker may be feasible." (§1)

> "The reason why the approach is more practical to implement is that it sidesteps completely the issue of whether the certifying algorithm is correctly implemented, which is difficult, by showing only that a particular output is correct, which is often easy." (§1)

Crucially the paper flags that simplicity alone is **not** sufficient — the checker must be *demonstrably correct*, or trust merely moves:

> "We want to stress that it does not suffice for the checker to be a simple algorithm. It is equally [important that the checker be correct]." (§1)

And the honest endpoint — a *verified* checker — has a documented obstruction (the checker must itself be written in a language with formal semantics):

> "Since checkers are so simple, we might be able to prove them correct. There is an obvious objection. Only programs written in a programming language with a formally defined semantics can be proven correct, but most algorithms are written in languages, e.g., C, C++ or Java, whose semantics are only informally defined." (§6.3)

**Bearing on Precept.** The certifying-algorithm frame fits exactly: the proof engine is the (untrusted) prover; each `ProofObligation` would carry a witness; a small checker re-verifies. The trusted base shrinks **iff** (a) the checker is small, (b) the checker is itself trustworthy (tested-to-exhaustion or verified), and (c) the witness is cheap to check. The paper makes (a)/(b)/(c) the explicit success conditions — they become this survey's evaluation axes.

### Closest analogue: validated vs. verified abstract interpretation (Verasco)

Precept *is* a small abstract interpreter. The two poles — "verify the whole analyzer" vs "check each run's result" — are both instantiated, in the *same tool*, by Verasco. Verasco is the verify-the-whole-engine pole [Primary; Jourdan, Laporte, Blazy, Leroy, Pichardie, *A Formally-Verified C Static Analyzer*, POPL 2015; access 2026-06-06; mirrored to `…/verasco-jourdan-laporte-blazy-leroy-pichardie-popl2015.txt`]:

> "This paper reports on the design and soundness proof, using the Coq proof assistant, of Verasco, a static analyzer based on abstract interpretation … not only the soundness of the analysis results is guaranteed with mathematical certitude, but also the fact that these guarantees carry over [to the executable]." (Abstract)

The soundness theorem is the no-false-proof property Precept wants — *if the analyzer reports no alarms, there are none*:

> "if the analyzer reports no alarms, it must be the [case that the program is safe]." (§1)

The cost of the verify-the-whole-engine pole is explicit and large:

> "The soundness proof for the abstract interpreter is massive …" (§6)

The decisive finding for Precept: **Verasco itself does not verify its hardest domain — it uses a-posteriori result validation with a checked certificate**, precisely the witness-checking architecture, and explains *why*:

> "VPL is implemented in Caml using GMP rational arithmetic, and therefore cannot be trusted. However, every operation produces a Farkas certificate that can easily be checked by a validator written and proved sound in Coq. For example, the join operation, applied to polyhedras P1, P2, returns not only a polyhedron P but also Farkas certificates proving that the system of inequations (P1 ∨ P2) ∧ ¬P is unsatisfiable." (§6.3)

> "This is, currently, the only instance of verified validation a posteriori in Verasco. While simpler abstract domains can be verified directly with reasonable proof effort, verified validation is a perfect match for this domain, avoiding proofs of difficult algorithms and enabling efficient implementations of costly polyhedral computations." (§6.3)

Verasco also sketches the *general* validation pattern for its fixpoint iterator — delegate to an untrusted engine, then check:

> "Alternatively, we could delegate the computation of a candidate post-fixpoint A to an untrusted iterator written in Caml, then check A ⊒ F A in a verified Coq function. This would cost one more invocation of the F function." (§5)

**Bearing on Precept.** This is the survey's strongest evidence. Verasco demonstrates the *engineering rule* directly applicable to Precept: **verify the cheap/simple domains directly; result-check the hard ones with a certificate.** The Farkas-certificate-for-polyhedra-join pattern is the canonical witness-checking instance for an abstract interpreter, and the witness (a Farkas non-negative linear combination) is exactly one of the legible witnesses the sibling survey identified. Verasco's own division of labor — "simple domains verified directly, hard domain validated a posteriori" — maps onto Precept's strategy taxonomy (most strategies are trivially-checkable; the relational/Karr ones want a certificate).

### Proof-carrying code and LCF: the asymmetry principle (checking ≪ proving)

PCC and the LCF/de Bruijn lineage both rest on the **generation–checking asymmetry**: producing a proof is hard (search), checking a proof is easy (re-execution of inference steps). PCC ships a safety proof with the code; the consumer's *proof validator* re-checks it; the trusted base is the validator plus the safety policy, not the (untrusted, possibly adversarial) producer [Tertiary — Necula & Lee, *Proof-Carrying Code*, POPL 1997 / *Safe Kernel Extensions Without Run-Time Checking*, OSDI 1996; primary fetch failed (403/404), claims restated from training-data + the certifying-algorithm frame; flagged in Threats]. The LCF discipline (Milner) makes the trusted base a *small kernel*: theorems can only be constructed through a narrow set of primitive-inference constructors on an abstract `thm` type, so any derivation — however elaborate the untrusted tactic that found it — is sound by construction because it factors through the kernel. The de Bruijn criterion generalizes this: a system satisfies it if proofs can be *independently checked by a small program* [Tertiary — de Bruijn / Automath lineage; HOL, Coq (the kernel re-checks proof terms), Isabelle/Pure; training-data, flagged].

**Bearing on Precept.** The asymmetry is the R2 lever: if checking is strictly cheaper than proving, a per-keystroke checker is affordable *given* the prover already ran. The LCF "narrow kernel" pattern is the architecture that makes the trusted base genuinely small — **the checker, not the prover, is the trusted artifact.** For Precept, the analogue is: the 10 strategies are untrusted tactics; a single small checker over the witness DU is the kernel.

### The certificate-format evidence: cheap-to-check is a *design property*, not automatic (DRAT vs Alethe)

The SAT/SMT proof-checking world supplies the hard R2 data point: **checking is cheap only if the certificate format is designed for it** — and some certificate formats are *not*. DRAT is the cheap-checking pole, by explicit design [Primary; Wetzler, Heule, Hunt, *The DRAT format and DRAT-trim checker*, 2016, arXiv:1610.06229; access 2026-06-06; mirrored to `…/drat-format-drat-trim-wetzler-heule-hunt-2016.txt`]:

> "The DRAT format and the corresponding checker DRAT-trim are designed based on the following goals: 1) It should be easy to emit clausal proofs … 2) proofs should be compact … 3) proof validation should be efficient; and 4) all techniques used in state-of-the-art SAT solvers should be expressible in the format." (§1)

Even so, DRAT validation needs real engineering to stay cheap — efficient checking is not free:

> "In order to efficiently validate a DRAT proof, several optimizations to the checking algorithm are required. Examples of such optimizations are backward checking and core first unit propagation." (§"Remarks on Efficient Proof Validation")

Alethe is the **counter-pole** — the honest cost case where a coarse-grained certificate forces the checker to *do reasoning*, so checking is *not* cheap and the trusted base does *not* shrink to a kernel [Primary; Barbosa, Blanchette, Fleury, Fontaine, *Alethe: Towards a Generic SMT Proof Format*, arXiv:2107.02354; access 2026-06-06; mirrored to `…/alethe-smt-proof-format-barbosa-blanchette-fleury-fontaine-arxiv2107.02354.txt`]:

> "[coarse-grained] proof steps in the proof and thus relying on powerful proof checkers capable of filling the gaps." (§1)

> "all the different levels of granularity must be allowed by the format, each requiring different complexity levels of checking." (§"Granularity")

> "the Farkas' coefficient of the linear arithmetic rule are provided as arguments. This puts pressure on proof checkers and reconstruction in proof assistants to support all the variants or at least the most general one (at the cost of efficiency)." (§"Granularity")

And the trusted-base-does-not-shrink admission, verbatim — Alethe's independent performance-oriented checker explicitly abandons the small-kernel guarantee:

> "In contrast to a proof-assistant-based reconstruction, this checker will not be structured around a small, trusted kernel … the user would need to trust the implementation does not lead to wrong checking results." (§"Powerful Tooling")

**Bearing on Precept.** This is the load-bearing R1+R2 caveat. *Fine-grained* certificates (DRAT-style: each step a primitive the checker re-executes) give cheap checking and a tiny kernel. *Coarse-grained* certificates (Alethe-style: a step says "by linear arithmetic" and the checker must re-derive the Farkas combination) push real reasoning into the checker — making checking expensive (R2 risk) and the checker large (no trusted-base shrink). **Which pole Precept lands on is a witness-design decision, not a given.** A witness that merely *names* the strategy ("proved by IntervalContainment") is coarse-grained and useless for checking; a witness that *carries the narrowed intervals and the containment arithmetic* is fine-grained and cheaply re-checkable.

### Translation validation: the per-run-vs-per-tool axis

Pnueli's translation validation validates *each compiler run* (does the output preserve the input's semantics?) rather than verifying the compiler once — trading a once-and-for-all proof for a per-run check that is simpler to build and re-runs every compilation [Tertiary — Pnueli, Siegel, Singerman, *Translation Validation*, TACAS 1998, LNCS 1384; training-data, flagged]. It is the same per-run/per-tool tradeoff Verasco's a-posteriori validation embodies, applied to compilation rather than analysis.

**Bearing on Precept.** Per-run checking is the right axis for a per-keystroke compiler: the checker re-runs every recompile, catching a *this-input* false proof even if the prover has a latent bug that only manifests on certain shapes. The tradeoff (you re-pay the check cost every run, vs. paying the verification cost once) is exactly R2's concern — affordable only if checking is cheap (the DRAT pole), risky if not (the Alethe pole).

## Threats to Validity

- **PCC, LCF/de Bruijn, and Pnueli legs are Tertiary (training-data).** The Necula PCC primary fetch failed (403 cs.cmu.edu, 404 berkeley.edu); the LCF small-kernel / de Bruijn-criterion and translation-validation claims are restated from training-data knowledge, not a fetched primary. These ground the **asymmetry principle** and the **small-kernel target** — both of which are *also* grounded by the Primary certifying-algorithm and Verasco/DRAT/Alethe excerpts, so no load-bearing conclusion rests on the Tertiary legs alone. Upgrade by fetching Necula POPL'97 and a HOL/Coq kernel reference before locking.
- **WebFetch hallucination caught and discarded.** The fetch model returned *fabricated* "verbatim" quotes for the DRAT and Alethe PDFs (it could not read the binary streams). Those were discarded; **every DRAT/Alethe/certifying-algorithm/Verasco excerpt in Findings is from the locally `pdftotext`-extracted mirror**, not the fetch model's prose. This is the same forcing-function the citation discipline intends — the excerpts were re-extracted from the mirrors, not trusted from the summarizer.
- **PDF text-extraction fidelity.** All Primary excerpts were extracted via `pdftotext`; the `[sic]` on "may cases" and "polyhedras" are extraction artifacts of the source PDFs, preserved verbatim. Mathematical notation (`⊒`, `∨`, subscripts) may render imperfectly; the prose excerpts quoted are robust.
- **Domain-mismatch caveat (DRAT/Alethe).** SAT/SMT certificates check *unsatisfiability of a formula*, not *an abstract-interpreter's per-obligation verdict*. They are cited for the **certificate-granularity-determines-checking-cost** principle (a general property of witness checking), not as a direct template for Precept's witness. The directly-templatable evidence is Verasco's Farkas-certificate-for-polyhedra (an abstract interpreter), which is Primary.
- **Generation-completeness is under-grounded.** The survey addresses the *soundness* side (no false `Proved`) well; the dual *coverage* side (every obligation that *should* be emitted *is* emitted — a missed obligation is the BUG-017 failure mode) has no external comparator here. Certifying algorithms check "this output is correct," not "all required outputs were produced" — coverage is a Precept-specific catalog-enumeration property (see Open Questions), not something witness-checking addresses. Flagged so the design pass does not over-claim.
- **Checker-trust regress is real, not hand-waved.** The certifying-algorithm paper itself stresses the checker must be correct, not merely simple (§6.3 objection). The "trusted base shrinks" conclusion is *conditional* on the checker being verifiable or exhaustively testable — if Precept's checker is as complex as the prover, the base merely *moves*. The conclusions state this conditionality explicitly rather than assuming the shrink.

## Implications for Precept

1. **The architecture fits the gap exactly — the engine is a certifying-algorithm candidate.** The proof engine is an untrusted prover; §0.6 #6 already requires each verdict to carry structured attribution. Witness-checking adds the missing third party: a small trusted checker that re-verifies that attribution. The motivating gap (`Proved` is self-reported, `[StaticallyPreventable]` is an unchecked correspondence) is precisely what a checker closes.
2. **Verasco gives Precept the engineering rule, not just the theory: verify-simple / validate-hard.** Most of Precept's 10 strategies are *trivially checkable* (Literal: re-evaluate the constant; IntervalContainment: re-check the assigned interval ⊆ declared interval; Length/Count: re-check the bound arithmetic). These need no certificate beyond the data they already compute — the checker re-runs the containment test. The *relational* strategies (FlowNarrowing, the future Karr/octagon work) are the "hard domain" where a **fine-grained certificate** (the narrowed-bound derivation chain / the Karr equality basis / the Farkas combination — all identified in the sibling survey as the witness each domain *naturally produces*) is the right move, exactly as Verasco validates polyhedra-join with Farkas certificates rather than verifying the join code.
3. **R1 is satisfied — and witness-checking *strengthens* §0.6 #3 rather than straining it.** The witnesses the sibling survey enumerated (equality basis, Farkas combination, congruence-closure k-step explanation, DBM derivation chain) are *already* the legible structured-data witnesses §0.6 #3 mandates. A checker that re-verifies them is the *consumer* §0.6 #3 implies but never named. The only R1 risk is the **Alethe trap**: a coarse witness that names the strategy without carrying the reasoning forces the checker to re-derive, which both costs R2 and bloats the trusted base. The witness must be fine-grained (DRAT-pole), carrying the arithmetic, not just the verdict.
4. **R2 holds for the trivial strategies and is a design constraint for the relational ones.** Re-checking interval/length/count containment is O(1)–O(n) per obligation — strictly cheaper than the narrowing that produced it (the generation–checking asymmetry), comfortably inside the ~3 ms worst-file budget. The relational checker cost depends on witness granularity: a Farkas-combination check is a single non-negativity verification (cheap); re-running octagon closure inside the checker would *not* be cheap (Alethe pole). The design must pick the cheap-to-check witness.
5. **The trusted base shrinks *if and only if* the checker is small and itself trustworthy.** The honest assessment: for the trivial strategies the checker is genuinely tiny (re-evaluate / re-contain) and the shrink is real — the 10 narrowing strategies become untrusted, the small containment checker becomes the kernel. For the relational strategies the checker is a Farkas/equality-basis verifier — still far smaller than the narrowing engine, but non-trivial. The base **shrinks substantially but does not vanish**, and the checker itself must then be the thing that is exhaustively tested or (aspirationally) verified — trust *concentrates*, it does not disappear.

## Conclusions

**C1 — Witness/certificate checking IS the right soundness mechanism for Precept under R1+R2 — adopt it as a checker over fine-grained per-obligation witnesses, not coarse strategy-name tags.**
- *Rationale.* It is the only surveyed mechanism that closes the exact gap (self-reported `Proved` → re-verified `Proved`) while *satisfying* R1 (the witnesses are already the §0.6 #3 legible structured data) and respecting R2 (checking is strictly cheaper than the narrowing that produced it — the generation–checking asymmetry — for fine-grained witnesses). It fits the engine's existing certifying shape: §0.6 #6 already mandates the witness; checking adds the trusted consumer.
- *Alternatives considered and rejected.* (a) **Verify the whole engine (Verasco pole)** — rejected as the *primary* mechanism: Verasco's own soundness proof is "massive," the C# engine has no formal semantics to verify against (the §6.3 objection), and it is disproportionate to a ~600-LOC engine; Verasco *itself* falls back to result-validation for its hard domain, which is the recommendation here. (b) **Heavy testing alone** — rejected as the *soundness guarantee*: testing samples inputs, a false proof is exhaustive-soundness's complement, and §0.6 #1 is a "must never" — testing cannot certify "never" over an infinite input space (it remains essential for the *checker*, see C3). (c) **Coarse strategy-name witnesses** — rejected: the Alethe evidence shows coarse certificates push reasoning into the checker, costing R2 and bloating the trusted base ("not structured around a small, trusted kernel").
- *Precedent.* Certifying Algorithms CSR'11 (witness + simple checker, trusted-base shrink conditional on checker simplicity+correctness); Verasco POPL'15 (Farkas-certificate a-posteriori validation of polyhedra-join as the abstract-interpreter instance); DRAT 2016 (fine-grained ⇒ efficiently validable) vs Alethe arXiv'21 (coarse ⇒ "pressure on proof checkers," no small kernel).
- *Tradeoff accepted.* A second artifact to build and maintain (the checker), a per-recompile checking pass re-paid every keystroke, and trust *concentrated* in (not eliminated from) the checker — which must itself become the exhaustively-tested/verified component. The prover's bugs become *detected* (the checker rejects), not *impossible*; soundness now rests on the checker, not the strategies.

**C2 — Apply the Verasco verify-simple / validate-hard split: trivial strategies get a re-execution checker (no certificate); relational strategies get a fine-grained certificate.**
- *Rationale.* Literal/IntervalContainment/Length/Count are cheaply *re-runnable* — the checker recomputes the containment from the data the engine already produced, needing no separate certificate (the witness *is* the recomputation). FlowNarrowing/Karr/octagon are where a derivation certificate (narrowed-bound chain / equality basis / Farkas combination) earns its keep, exactly as Verasco verifies simple domains directly and validates polyhedra a posteriori. This minimizes both checker size and per-run cost.
- *Alternatives considered and rejected.* (a) **One uniform certificate format for all strategies** — rejected: over-engineers the trivial strategies (a certificate for "5 ∈ [0,10]" is pure overhead) and risks the Alethe coarse-granularity trap if forced to a common shape. (b) **Re-run the full narrowing inside the checker for relational strategies** — rejected: that re-pays the expensive O(n³) closure per keystroke (Alethe pole), defeating R2; check the *certificate* (cheap), not the *computation*.
- *Precedent.* Verasco §6.3 ("simpler abstract domains can be verified directly … verified validation is a perfect match for [the hard] domain"); the sibling `solver-free-static-analysis-techniques-survey.md` (each domain's natural witness — Karr basis, Farkas combination, congruence-closure explanation, DBM chain).
- *Tradeoff accepted.* Two checking idioms (re-execution vs certificate-check) instead of one, and the relational certificates must be threaded through the narrowing code that produces them — a coupling between prover and witness-emission that the trivial strategies avoid.

**C3 — The trusted base shrinks substantially but does not vanish; the checker becomes the concentrated trust anchor and must be the exhaustively-tested/verified component — and witness-checking does NOT address generation-completeness, which stays a separate Precept-specific obligation.**
- *Rationale.* Honest accounting: for the trivial strategies the shrink is real and large (10 narrowing strategies → one small containment checker). For the relational strategies the checker is a certificate-verifier — smaller than the prover but non-trivial. Soundness then rests on the checker, so the checker (not the strategies) is what must be tested to exhaustion or verified (the CSR'11 §6.3 "it does not suffice for the checker to be simple … it must be correct"). Separately, witness-checking certifies "each emitted `Proved` is justified," **not** "every obligation that should be emitted was" — a missed obligation (BUG-017 shape) is a *coverage* hole the checker is blind to. Coverage is a catalog-enumeration property (does every fault-prone construct instantiate its obligation?), distinct from witness-soundness.
- *Alternatives considered and rejected.* (a) **Claim the trusted base "disappears"** — rejected as dishonest: trust concentrates in the checker, it does not vanish (CSR'11 stresses checker correctness; Alethe's performance checker explicitly re-introduces implementation trust). (b) **Fold coverage into witness-checking** — rejected: certifying algorithms check output-correctness, not output-completeness; coverage needs its own mechanism (a catalog-driven enumeration audit that every `[StaticallyPreventable]` fault has a live obligation generator — cf. the MEMORY "enumerate the whole family" discipline).
- *Precedent.* CSR'11 §1/§6.3 (trusted-base shrink conditional on checker correctness; the verify-the-checker objection); Alethe "the user would need to trust the implementation"; Precept's own PRECEPT0001/0002 analyzers check *routing*, not *soundness* — the existing coverage-vs-soundness split.
- *Tradeoff accepted.* Precept must build/maintain a checker *and* a separate coverage audit; the guarantee becomes "the checker would have caught a false proof on this input, and the coverage audit ensures the obligation exists" — two mechanisms, two trusted components, rather than one verified monolith. The payoff is that a prover bug is *caught* rather than *trusted away*, at the cost of the checker becoming load-bearing.

## What would change these conclusions

- **C1 falsifier.** If a probe of the relational strategies shows their *natural* witness is irreducibly coarse — i.e. checking it requires re-running the narrowing (no Farkas-style certificate that is cheaper to check than to produce exists for Precept's single-pass depth-1 closure) — then witness-checking lands on the Alethe pole for the strategies that most need it, R2 fails, and the recommendation narrows to "checker for the trivial strategies only, verify-or-test the relational ones directly."
- **C1/C2 falsifier.** If profiling shows the re-execution checker for the trivial strategies roughly *doubles* per-keystroke compile time (the asymmetry is weak because narrowing is already cheap), the "checking ≪ proving" premise is too thin to justify the second pass, and direct heavy-testing of the prover becomes the better cost trade.
- **C3 falsifier (shrink).** If the relational certificate-checker turns out to need most of the relational domain logic to verify (e.g. checking a Karr equality basis requires the same Gaussian elimination that produced it), the trusted base *moves* rather than shrinks, and the verify-the-whole-relational-domain pole (Verasco-style, for that domain only) becomes competitive.
- **C3 falsifier (coverage).** If a mechanical coverage audit (every fault-prone catalog construct ⇒ a live obligation generator) proves *as hard as* witness-checking or subsumes it, the "two separate mechanisms" framing collapses into one and C3's split is wrong.
- **Spec falsifier.** If a future §0.6 edit mandates re-verification (closing the "who checks the witness" gap at the language level), the architecture question moves from research to spec and this survey is superseded by the canonical decision.

## Open Questions

- **Does each relational strategy admit a fine-grained, cheaper-to-check-than-to-produce certificate within §0.4's single-pass envelope?** Requires a `ProofEngine.Intervals.cs` probe (the same probe the sibling survey flags for Karr): does `RelationalHalfLine`/`BuildNarrowedIntervals` produce a derivation chain that a checker can re-verify without re-running the closure? This is the load-bearing R2 question for the relational tier.
- **What is the checker's verification story — tested-to-exhaustion, or formally verified?** CSR'11 §6.3 flags that verifying a checker needs a language with formal semantics; C# is not that. Is property-based exhaustive testing of the (small) checker the realistic assurance, or is a checker-in-a-verified-fragment worth it? Needs an owner decision at design time.
- **Coverage/generation-completeness mechanism.** Is "every `[StaticallyPreventable]` fault has a live, non-stubbed obligation generator" mechanically checkable from the catalog (a Roslyn-analyzer-style audit), or is it a Precept-specific enumeration property requiring a bespoke pass? The `DiagnosticCoverageScanner` (MEMORY: dynamic-dispatch emission sites) is the nearest existing machinery — does it generalize to obligation-generation coverage?
- **Where does the checker run in the pipeline?** A post-proof checking stage (re-verify the `ProofLedger` before it leaves the proof engine) vs. an always-on assertion in debug builds vs. a separate conformance harness. The per-keystroke-LS path constrains this (R2); a debug-only checker sidesteps R2 but weakens the guarantee in release.
- **Does the existing conformance-suite work (`docs/Working/conformance-suite-*.md`) overlap the checker role?** A conformance suite tests the engine against expected verdicts; a checker re-verifies each run's witness. They are complementary (suite = offline coverage of known cases; checker = online soundness of every case) but the boundary should be drawn explicitly.
